# Patch, Matrix and Corridor

Picture a farm map: a big spread of soybean fields, and inside it a small meadow, a woodlot and a pond, with a line of trees running along a creek from the woodlot to a forest on the far side. The meadow, woodlot and pond are *patches*: fairly uniform areas that differ from what surrounds them. The soybean fields are the *matrix*: the most widespread, most connected cover, the background everything else sits in. The creek trees are a *corridor*: a strip that links patches and lets some animals, seeds or water move between them. This **patch-matrix-corridor model** is the basic vocabulary of [[Landscape Ecology]], because it turns a messy map into three questions: how big and how far apart are the patches, how hostile is the matrix, and what do the corridors connect?

Why these questions? Small patches hold fewer individuals and lose more of their area to edge ([[Edge Effects]], [[Area Sensitivity]]); a patch far from others is hard to recolonise if its birds disappear one year; a meadow surrounded by pasture is less isolated, for a grassland bird, than one surrounded by forest; and corridors cut both ways, since a hedgerow helps a woodland bird move but splits a meadow and gives predators a perch. The key point is that what counts as patch, matrix or corridor depends on which species is asking. Take a 10 × 10 grid of land cells: 60 forest, 30 grassland in three blobs, 8 crop field and 2 cells of tree line linking two blobs. For a grassland bird, forest covers 60 of 100 cells, so it is the (hostile) matrix, crops are a gentler matrix, the grass blobs are its [[Habitat Patch|habitat patches]] and the tree line is a barrier. Flip to a woodland warbler and the forest becomes its [[Habitat]], the grass blobs become gaps and the tree line a corridor. Same map, different reading.

Three confusions: the matrix is not empty space (it can feed predators, shade edges and supply tree seeds that invade patches, [[Woody Encroachment]]); corridors are not always good; and a patch is not necessarily grassland but whatever cover you are focusing on, so choose the species first, then label the map. The grassland bird data release describes every site as "patchy remnant grasslands interspersed within a forested or agricultural matrix" ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]): grassland pieces are the patches the six [[Focal Species]] need, forest (and in places [[Land-Use Change and Agriculture|row crops]]) is the matrix, and wooded strips along ditches and fence lines create the tree-grass boundaries that [[Spatial Covariance]] detects as negative values. [[Berry et al. 2026]] asked whether birds sit in the cores of those patches, away from the forest matrix ([[Grassland Core Habitat]]), and the field data record how much matrix intrudes within 50 m of each point, such as woodland (WD50), forest (FO50) and scrub (SC50) ([[Percent Cover]]). Breaking patches apart is [[Habitat Fragmentation]].
