# Roadside Surveys

Picture a country road at dawn. A truck stops, the observer steps out, does a [[Point Count Survey]] for a few minutes, drives on and stops again. In one morning they can do dozens of stops without wading through wet grass or getting lost. That is the appeal of a **roadside survey**: access, since roads go where people can reach and the counts are fast. Big programs need many points cheaply, and the [[Breeding Bird Survey]], the largest bird monitoring program in North America, is built entirely on roadside stops. But look at what lines the road: a ditch, a fence, a power line, maybe a tree row, [[Mowing|mowed]] verges, gravel and traffic noise. The strip next to a road is not a typical piece of the field behind it; it is an edge, with all the [[Edge Effects]] that brings.

The trade-off is representativeness. Fences and wires are perches, so kingbirds, [[Scissor-tailed Flycatcher|Scissor-tailed Flycatchers]] and shrikes that hunt from them may be drawn to roadsides. Brushy ditches and tree rows suit [[Habitat Generalist|generalists]] such as Indigo Buntings and Northern Cardinals ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]). Traffic noise drowns quiet songs, lowering the [[Detection Probability]]. And roads usually run on drier ground away from a patch's interior, under-sampling [[Grassland Core Habitat]]. Try made-up counts at two points on one site. An interior grid point records DICK, [[Eastern Meadowlark|EAME]], RWBL and [[Northern Bobwhite|NOBO]]: 4 species, 3 of them grassland [[Focal Species|focal species]]. A roadside point records INBU, NOCA, STFL, AMCR, FISP and YBCH: 6 species, only 1 focal. The roadside point has more species but fewer [[Habitat Specialist|grassland specialists]], so a site with many roadside points can look rich for reasons that have nothing to do with its grassland ([[Why all-species and focal-species richness can disagree]]). None of this makes roadside counts useless; it means they answer a slightly different question, and mixing them with interior points needs care.

Three confusions: more species at roadside points does not mean roads are good habitat, only that they are edges where two habitats meet; roadside and grid points are not interchangeable, since they follow different placement rules ([[Systematic Grid Sampling]]); and the BBS is not flawed for using roads, since its strength is consistency over decades, and trends stay useful as long as roadsides change like the wider landscape. In the grassland bird habitat file the `Roadside` column (1 roadside, 0 interior) shows how different the sites are: BK 67 of 96 points roadside (70%), CR 0 of 47 (0%), DC 52 of 93 (56%), HB 75 of 84 (89%), HO 44 of 96 (46%). Holla Bend is almost all roadside and Camp Robinson has none, one more way `Site` bundles many differences together ([[Confounding]], [[Why site identity is a weak test of a habitat hypothesis]]). The detailed field vegetation measures were mostly not collected at roadside points, which explains many of the [[Missing Values|missing values]] ([[Field vs Remote Vegetation Measures]]), and `Roadside` could itself be a [[Predictor Variable]] or [[Detection Covariates|detection covariate]], an idea to raise with Dr. Roberts ([[Open Questions]], [[Roberts et al. 2025]]).
