# Theme and Key Points

Think of a necklace: the theme is the string, and sentences and paragraphs are beads; a bead that is not on the string is just lying on the table. A paper's **theme** is the single thread running through it, carried by two or three points you want every reader to remember, and anything that does not serve them should be cut. McCarthy's advice (Savage and Yeh 2019) is to decide on your theme and those two or three points first, and to write each down in one sentence before drafting ([[McCarthy Writing Advice]]).

Then test every paragraph: does it help the reader understand the theme? If not, omit it ([[One Idea per Paragraph]], [[Cutting Unnecessary Words]]). For a modelling paper the points are usually the ecological answer, its size and uncertainty, and what it means ([[Effect Size]]). The abstract, the last paragraph of the introduction, the first sentences of the results and the first paragraph of the discussion should all state the same points ([[Writing Results Sections]]).

This is from the November 24 writing-results session and applies directly to the [[Final Project]] ([[Course Website]]). Not every analysis you ran belongs in the paper: only what serves the theme, and the rest goes to supplements or the bin.
