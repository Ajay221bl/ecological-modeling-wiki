# Why trees take over grasslands without fire
> [!summary] In plain words
> In a wet climate like Arkansas, the "default" vegetation is forest. Grass stays dominant only where something keeps killing young trees, and historically that something was mostly fire (helped by grazing). Grass feeds fire and fire protects grass, so the two hold each other in place. Remove fire long enough and saplings grow too big to kill, shade out the grass, and the loop flips to favour trees.

## The question
An abandoned lawn in Arkansas becomes a thicket, then a young forest, within a few decades. A prairie in the same climate can stay open for centuries. Why the difference, and why do all the study sites need mowing, burning or disking?

- [[Disturbance]]: events like fire that knock that sequence back.
- [[Prescribed Fire]]: the managed version of the historic disturbance.

## The reasoning, step by step
1. **Plants compete for light.** Trees are the tallest plants, so wherever they can grow and nothing stops them, they eventually win.
2. **Arkansas can grow trees.** Annual rain at the study sites is 127 to 137 cm, and summers are long and warm. That is plenty for forest. Unlike the dry western plains, grasslands here are not held open by drought; they need some other brake ([[Biome]], [[Grassland]]).
3. **Succession is the default.** On an unmanaged field, the usual sequence is grasses and weeds, then shrubs and briars, then fast-growing trees, then a closed forest ([[Ecological Succession]]).
4. **Fire is the brake.** A grass fire is low and fast. It kills seedlings and saplings, whose bark is thin and whose buds sit inside the flames. Grasses survive because their growing points sit at or below the soil, so they regrow within weeks. Historically, lightning and burning by Native American peoples set many fires, and grazing animals such as bison and elk added pressure.
5. **The loop.** Grass dries into fine fuel, fuel carries fire, fire kills young trees, and fewer trees leave more sun for grass. Each piece supports the next. This is a self-reinforcing (positive) feedback ([[Feedback Loops in Ecology]]).
6. **Timing decides who wins.** A sapling needs a run of fire-free years to grow thick bark and lift its crown out of the flame zone. Made-up numbers: suppose a sapling must survive 5 fire-free years. If fire comes in a given year with chance 0.5, the chance of 5 clear years is 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.03, so about 3 in 100 saplings escape. If fire comes with chance 0.1, it is 0.9 to the fifth power = 0.59, so about 59 in 100 escape ([[Probability]]).
7. **Then the loop flips.** Once enough trees escape, their shade thins the grass, so there is less fuel, so fires are weaker and rarer, so even more trees escape. Now the feedback favours trees. A match alone no longer restores the grassland; it may take cutting ([[Ecological Resilience and Alternative States]], [[Regime Shifts and Spatial Early Warning Signals]]).
8. **Mowing and disking are partial substitutes.** [[Mowing]] cuts stems, but many woody plants resprout from roots. [[Disking]] breaks up roots and soil, resetting succession to bare ground.

## So in my project
- All five sites are remnant grassland patches inside a forested or agricultural [[Patch, Matrix and Corridor|matrix]], so seeds and saplings arrive from all sides ([[Study Sites]]).
- The data release says every site has had restoration including mowing, burning and disking, in varying amounts ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]).
- How management differs from site to site (from a summary of Berry's 2024 thesis, still to be verified) is listed once, in [[Study Sites]].
- These management differences are one more thing that the `Site` label bundles together ([[Confounding]]). The tree signal they leave is what [[Spatial Covariance]] picks up from above.
- The cost shows up in my results: focal grassland richness was about 45% lower per SD of tree cover within 50 m (rate ratio 0.55; [[Preliminary Results]]). That is what letting trees win would mean for these six birds.

## What would go wrong otherwise
- Treating the grassland as a fixed backdrop would miss that management holds it open.
- Reading tree cover as "just another habitat feature" would miss its direction: trees, once established, push the system further toward trees.
- Recommending "add fire" everywhere would ignore that places past the tipping point need tree removal first ([[Why defend the core instead of restoring everywhere]]).
