# Grassland Specialists vs Generalists

> [!summary] In plain words
> A grassland specialist needs open grass; a generalist can live almost anywhere. When trees and shrubs move into a grassland, specialists leave and generalists arrive, so the total number of species can go up while the grassland gets worse. That is why the project models focal grassland species alongside all-species richness.

## The question
Why can a richer bird list mean a worse grassland, and how do the project's data show it?

## Built from
- [[Habitat Specialist]]: a species that needs one narrow kind of habitat
- [[Habitat Generalist]]: a species that can use many kinds of habitat
- [[Ecological Niche]]: why some species are narrow and others broad
- [[Edge Effects]]: changes near the border where grassland meets trees
- [[Species Richness]]: the count that treats every species alike

## The reasoning, step by step
1. **Specialists lose when grassland shrinks or fills with trees,** because it is their only home ([[Woody Encroachment]]); this is part of why grassland birds have declined more than most groups ([[Grassland Bird Decline]]).
2. **Generalists often gain at the same time,** because edges and shrubs suit them.
3. **So total richness can rise as a grassland degrades.** *Why:* richness counts every species equally and does not ask which kind ([[Edge Effects]]).
4. **"Specialist" depends on the habitat.** A Yellow-breasted Chat is a shrubland specialist; in a grassland study it counts as a non-grassland bird and signals shrubs moving in.
5. **It is a sliding scale.** Northern Bobwhite needs grass but also shrubby cover, so it sits in the middle. The obligate/facultative split of grassland birds is usually traced to Vickery et al. (1999); check that source's placements before quoting them.

## So in practice
The most detected species in the project data (point-visits out of 1,250, ground detections) are mostly not grassland birds: Indigo Bunting 863 (edge and shrub), Northern Cardinal 685 (generalist), Red-winged Blackbird 501 (marsh and field generalist), American Crow 481 (generalist), Yellow-breasted Chat 466 (shrub specialist), Common Yellowthroat 366 (wet shrub and tall grass), Tufted Titmouse 350 (woodland), then Dickcissel 331 (grassland, focal), Field Sparrow 288 (old field and shrubby grassland) and Northern Bobwhite 202 (grassland and brush, focal). The sites tell the same story on the 376 model points: BK has the lowest all-species richness (4.73) but the most [[Focal Species]] per visit (0.99), while CR has the highest richness (7.58) but among the fewest focal species (0.25). The models agree: all-species richness rose toward tree-grass edges, while focal richness rose toward cores and fell about 45% per SD of tree cover ([[Spatial Covariance]], [[Preliminary Results]]).

## What would go wrong otherwise
- Counting only total species would rank the worse grassland as better, rewarding exactly the change managers worry about.
- Calling generalists "bad birds" misses the point: they just do not tell you about grassland quality.
- Treating every bird in a field as a grassland bird ignores the many visitors from the edge.
