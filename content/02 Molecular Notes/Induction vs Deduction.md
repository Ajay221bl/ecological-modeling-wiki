# Induction vs Deduction

> [!summary] In plain words
> **Deduction** goes from a general rule to a particular case, and if the rule is true the conclusion must be. **Induction** goes from particular cases to a general rule, so the conclusion could be wrong. Science uses both in a loop: deduce what a hypothesis predicts, then induce from data whether it holds. Statistics is the tool that measures how far an inductive leap might be off.

## The everyday picture
Deduction: "All my keys are on this ring; this key isn't on the ring; so it isn't mine." Certain. Induction: "Every swan I've seen is white, so all swans are white." Reasonable, until you visit Australia and see a black one.

*Where the picture stops working:* in ecology even the "rules" deduction starts from are usually hypotheses, so its certainty is only as good as the premise.

## The question
How do induction and deduction differ, and how do they work together in science?

## Built from
- [[Induction]]: cases to general rule; uncertain
- [[Deduction]]: general rule to case; certain if the premises are
- [[Hypothesis]]: the rule being tested

## The reasoning, step by step
1. **Start with a hypothesis** ([[Hypothesis]]).
2. **Deduce a prediction** from it ([[Deduction]]). *Why:* a testable prediction is what lets data speak to the idea.
3. **Collect a sample and generalise** ([[Induction]]). *Why uncertain:* the sample isn't the whole population.
4. **Measure the uncertainty** with a confidence or credible interval ([[Confidence Interval]]).
5. **Loop:** the result supports or challenges the hypothesis, and you deduce the next prediction.

**A worked example in numbers.** Deduction: if a population of 200 is 40% female, it has 0.4 × 200 = **80** females, for certain. Induction: catch 50 animals, 20 female, and conclude "about 40% female", with a 95% CI of about **27% to 55%**. The interval is the honest size of the inductive leap.

## So in practice
State what your hypothesis predicts before collecting data, then report your estimates with intervals, never as bare numbers.

## What would go wrong otherwise
- You'd treat a sample result as certain.
- You'd collect data without knowing what result would count against your idea.
