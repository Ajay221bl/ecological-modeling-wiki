# Observational vs Experimental Study

> [!summary] In plain words
> in an experiment the researcher assigns treatments, ideally at random, so differences can be attributed to the treatment, while in an observational study the researcher only measures what already varies, so confounders can explain differences.

## The question
When can I claim an effect is causal?

## Built from
- [[Experimental Study]] and [[Observational Study]].
- [[Randomization]], [[Replication]], [[Control Group]], [[Treatment]].
- [[Confounding|Confounding Variable]].

## The reasoning, step by step
1. **Experiment:** you control X, randomise it, replicate, and keep a control.
2. **Observational:** nature sets X; you choose where and when to measure.
3. **Causal strength:** experiments are stronger because randomisation balances confounders.
4. **Scope:** observational studies can cover large areas, long times and things you cannot manipulate (climate, fire history).
5. **Both need good design:** representative samples, independent replicates, measured covariates.

| | Experimental | Observational |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets X | Researcher | Nature |
| Randomisation | Yes | No |
| Causal claims | Strong | Weak, needs confounder reasoning |
| Scale and realism | Often small | Often large |

## What would go wrong otherwise
Calling a comparison of burned and unburned sites "a fire experiment" when the fires were not assigned.
