# McGill 2013

> [!summary] In plain words
> A short blog essay arguing that in ecology nearly everything is more similar to things close by, in space or time. Instead of only treating that as a statistical nuisance, the author says it can also be useful information about what is going on. It matters as a friendly counterweight to the idea that nearby survey points are only a problem.

**Citation:** McGill, B. 2013. Autocorrelation: friend or foe? *Dynamic Ecology* (blog), posted 2 October 2013. https://dynamicecology.wordpress.com/2013/10/02/autocorrelation-friend-or-foe/

**Syllabus week:** Thur Oct 1, "Autocorrelation lecture and lab" (listed as Dynamic Ecology blog: "Autocorrelation: friend or foe?").

## What it's about
A short essay arguing that autocorrelation is a universal fact in ecology (Tobler's "near things are more related than distant things"), and that instead of only treating it as a statistical nuisance we should use it as information about processes and as a benchmark for our predictions.

## Key ideas I use
- Everything in ecology is autocorrelated in space, time and phylogeny; formally corr(x_i, x_j) is not zero, usually shrinking with distance (PDF p. 1) -> [[Spatial Autocorrelation]]
- Stationarity and isotropy are the usual simplifying assumptions; GLS handles the correlation by estimating a full error covariance matrix (PDF p. 1) -> [[Spatial Autocorrelation]] (remedies). Note: this "error covariance" is a statistical idea, not the grass-tree [[Spatial Covariance]] metric used in my habitat data.
- Four attitudes: unimportant (ignore it), foe (it violates the independence of errors), friend (its scale and shape reveal dispersal, environmental and other processes), and an ignorance measure or null model to beat (PDF pp. 2-3) -> [[Autocorrelation - Friend or Foe]], [[Independence Assumption]], [[Null Model]]
- Explicitly mentions the debate in [[Diniz-Filho et al. 2003]] (PDF p. 3)
