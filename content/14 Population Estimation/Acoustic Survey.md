# Acoustic Survey

You often hear a bird long before you see it, and some frogs are almost only ever heard. An **acoustic survey** detects a species from its sounds, by listening at points or along routes, or by recording ([[Occupancy]], [[Imperfect Detection]]). The guest lecture lists birds, vocal mammals, frogs and insects, and two classic forms: point counts, where an observer stands at a point for a fixed time and records every species heard or seen, and howl surveys, where surveyors play or imitate a howl and listen for wolves or coyotes to answer.

Point counts "can measure abundance and presence data": the same visit gives a count for an [[N-mixture Model]] or a detection for occupancy. Detection changes with time of day, season, wind, rain and observer hearing, which go in p as [[Observation Covariates]]; the classic advice to survey at dawn on calm days is about raising p. The lecture's spotted owl example is an acoustic design: two surveys at 32 sites, with wind speed, cloud cover, date and time of day recorded as detection covariates ([[Single-season Occupancy]]).

Recording devices now do much of the listening ([[Autonomous Recording Unit]], [[Occupancy Guest Lecture]], [[Choosing a Survey Method for Occupancy]]). A species that is silent is not absent: many animals call only in the breeding season or at certain hours, so surveys outside those times have p close to zero ([[Availability for Detection]]).
