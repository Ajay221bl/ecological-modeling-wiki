# Brood Parasitism

Imagine someone leaving their baby on your doorstep for you to raise, while your own children go hungry because the newcomer is bigger and louder. **Brood parasitism** is that strategy in birds: one bird, the parasite, lays its eggs in another species' nest, and the host raises the parasite's young, often at the cost of its own. In North America the main brood parasite is the Brown-headed Cowbird, which lays in the nests of more than a hundred host species, including many grassland birds such as the Dickcissel. It is one kind of interaction between members of an [[Ecological Community]] (the species living together in one place), closest to parasitism.

Follow one nest. A Dickcissel lays 4 eggs; a cowbird slips in, often removes a host egg and adds one of its own. The cowbird egg tends to hatch sooner and the chick grows faster, so it wins more of the food the parents bring, and the host may fledge fewer young of its own, sometimes none. Multiply that across a field and parasitism lowers how many young the host population raises each year.

Why does it matter for grassland birds? Cowbirds find nests by watching from perches, so trees and fences at a grassland's edge give them lookout posts, one of the ways edges hurt ground-nesting birds ([[Edge Effects]], [[Why grassland birds avoid trees]], [[Woody Encroachment]]). A common confusion is to think the host recognises the strange egg: many hosts do not, which is why the strategy works.
