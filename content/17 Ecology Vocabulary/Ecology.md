# Ecology

Stand at the edge of a hayfield in June. A [[Eastern Meadowlark|meadowlark]] sings from a fence post. Walk 200 m into the woods next door and it is gone; you hear a cardinal and a titmouse instead. Why does the meadowlark live in the field and not the woods? What would happen to it if the field grew into woods? Asking and answering questions like these is **ecology**: the science of where living things are, how many there are and why, worked out by studying how organisms interact with each other and with their surroundings. An *organism* is any single living thing: one bird, one grass plant, one beetle. Ecology always involves organisms plus something else, either other organisms (food, predators, competitors) or the non-living world (rain, soil, fire, sunlight). The word comes from the Greek *oikos*, "household": ecology studies nature's household, who lives where and how they get along.

Nature is not random: some places have many species and some few, some populations grow and others shrink, and a pattern is a clue that something is causing it. Ecology turns guesses about those causes into tests, using the same loop as any science. Observe a pattern (fewer meadowlarks near trees); propose a cause, a [[Hypothesis]] (trees give predators perches, an [[Edge Effects|edge effect]]); predict what you should see if the cause is real (meadowlarks get rarer as tree cover rises, even inside one site); then collect data and test it, usually with statistics. Try it small: count meadowlarks at an open field (4), a field with a hedge through it (1) and a woodlot (0). The pattern is fewer meadowlarks as woody cover rises, but is it the trees themselves, or something that comes with trees, such as less grass or more predators? And three places are far too few to be sure, so you would count at many places across a range of tree cover ([[Population and Sample]]), and remember that a pattern alone does not prove a cause ([[Correlation vs Causation]]).

Three confusions. Ecology is not environmentalism: environmentalism is a set of values about protecting nature, ecology is a science that measures and tests, though it often informs conservation. Ecology is not just describing nature: description is the start, and the goal is to explain causes and predict. And ecology is not the same as an [[Ecosystem]], which is one thing ecologists study. The grassland bird project is an ecology question: why does the number of bird species differ among points in five Arkansas grassland sites, and is habitat the reason ([[Study Sites]])? The statistics ([[Statistical Model]], [[GLMM]]) are tools; the ecology says what to put in the model and how to read the answer. Ecology says trees near a grassland matter to grassland birds ([[Woody Encroachment]]), so tree cover and [[Spatial Covariance]] went in as [[Predictor Variable|predictors]] instead of relying on site names alone, and in the Oct 13 results focal grassland [[Species Richness|richness]] was about 45% lower per SD of tree cover ([[Why site identity is a weak test of a habitat hypothesis]], [[Preliminary Results]]). The next ideas to learn are how ecologists slice nature into levels ([[Levels of Ecological Organization]]), what a species is ([[Species and Scientific Names]]), and [[Habitat]] and [[Biodiversity]].
