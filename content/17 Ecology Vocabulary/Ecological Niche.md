# Ecological Niche
**In one line:** A species' niche is its way of making a living: the range of conditions it can tolerate, the resources it uses, and the role it plays among other species.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Habitat]] (where a species lives; niche is about how) · [[Ecological Community]] (niches only make sense next to other species)

## Start from zero
If habitat is a species' **address**, its niche is its **job**. Two people can live on the same street (same address) and work as a baker and a plumber (different jobs). They do not compete for customers.

Ecologists describe a niche along several "axes" (directions you can measure):
- **What it eats:** seeds, insects, mice.
- **How it gets food:** probing the ground, catching insects in mid-air, hunting from a perch.
- **What conditions it tolerates:** how much tree cover, how tall the grass, how hot or wet.
- **When it is active:** season, time of day.

Put the tolerable range on each axis together and you get the niche: the "space" of conditions in which the species can persist.

Two refinements:
- **Fundamental niche:** everything it *could* use.
- **Realised niche:** what it *does* use once competitors and predators push it out of some places. Usually smaller.

## Why it exists
**1. It explains who lives together.** If two species had exactly the same niche, the better competitor would eventually push the other out of that place. This idea is called the **competitive exclusion principle**. So species that share a place usually differ somehow: different food, different height, different timing.

**2. It predicts responses to change.** If you know a species needs grass with almost no trees, you can predict it will decline when trees spread ([[Woody Plant Encroachment]]). If its niche includes some shrubs, it may cope better.

## A tiny example
Tolerance to tree cover within 50 m (made-up but realistic ranges):

| Species | Tree cover it tolerates |
|---|---|
| Eastern Meadowlark | 0 to 10% |
| Dickcissel | 0 to 15% |
| Northern Bobwhite | 5 to 40% (uses shrubby cover) |
| Northern Cardinal | 20 to 80% |

A point with 25% tree cover sits **outside** the meadowlark's and Dickcissel's ranges, **inside** the bobwhite's, and at the low end of the cardinal's. One habitat measurement, four different predictions.

## In my project
- **Niche overlap justifies pooling species.** [[Berry et al. 2026]] modeled six [[Focal Species]] together because all are grassland-dependent, so their niches overlap broadly. The model's [[Ecological Similarity Assumption]] relies on this.
- **But niches are not identical.** Two of the six, the Scissor-tailed Flycatcher and Eastern Kingbird, both catch flying insects from perches. They need *some* perches (a fence, a lone tree), unlike a meadowlark, which nests and feeds in the grass. The Loggerhead Shrike is a small predator that hunts from perches and stores prey on thorns or barbed wire ([[Loggerhead Shrike]]). The Northern Bobwhite needs shrubby cover for its family groups (coveys) ([[Northern Bobwhite]]).

The data release says boundary avoidance was "idiosyncratic across species" ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]): some of the six avoided tree-grass boundaries strongly, while bobwhite, which uses woody-open mixes, avoided them least (the species-by-species list, still to be verified, is in [[Focal Species]]). These differences are exactly what niche differences would predict.

## Common confusions
- **"Niche = place."** No. A niche is a set of needs and a role. The place is the [[Habitat]].
- **"Specialist = narrow niche, so always rare."** A narrow niche can still be common where conditions fit, as Dickcissels are in Bald Knob's open mix of grass and crop fields.
- **"Same family = same niche."** The kingbird and the flycatcher are close relatives with similar but not identical needs.
- **"Empty niche" in everyday speech** means a market gap. In ecology it is rarely that simple.
