# Ecological Niche

If [[Habitat]] (the place a species lives) is a species' address, its niche is its job. Two people can live on the same street and work as a baker and a plumber, so they never compete for customers. An **ecological niche** is a species' way of making a living: the range of conditions it can tolerate, the resources it uses and the role it plays among the other species of its [[Ecological Community]] (all the species living together in one place). Ecologists describe a niche along several measurable "axes": what it eats (seeds, insects, mice), how it gets food (probing the ground, catching insects in mid-air, hunting from a perch), what conditions it tolerates (tree cover, grass height, heat, wetness) and when it is active. Put the tolerable range on every axis together and you get the space of conditions in which the species can persist. The *fundamental niche* is everything it could use; the *realised niche* is what it actually uses once competitors and predators push it out of some places, and it is usually smaller.

Build it on one axis. Here is tolerance to tree cover within 50 m for four birds (made-up but realistic ranges): [[Eastern Meadowlark]] 0 to 10%, Dickcissel 0 to 15%, Northern Bobwhite 5 to 40% (it uses shrubby cover), Northern Cardinal 20 to 80%. A point with 25% tree cover sits outside the meadowlark's and Dickcissel's ranges, inside the bobwhite's and at the low end of the cardinal's: one habitat measurement, four different predictions. That is the first use of the niche idea, predicting how species respond to change: a bird that needs grass with almost no trees should decline as trees spread ([[Woody Encroachment]]). The second use is explaining who lives together. If two species had exactly the same niche, the better competitor would eventually push the other out of that place (the *competitive exclusion principle*), so species that share a place usually differ somehow, in food, height or timing.

Four confusions. A niche is not a place: it is a set of needs and a role, while the place is the habitat. A narrow niche does not mean always rare: Dickcissels are common where conditions fit, as in Bald Knob's open mix of grass and crop fields. Close relatives do not share a niche: [[Eastern Kingbird]] and [[Scissor-tailed Flycatcher]] have similar but not identical needs. And the everyday "empty niche" (a market gap) is rarely that simple in ecology. In the grassland bird project, [[Berry et al. 2026]] modelled six [[Focal Species]] together because all are grassland-dependent and their niches overlap broadly, the basis of the [[Ecological Similarity Assumption]]. But the niches are not identical: the flycatcher and kingbird catch flying insects from perches and so need some perches; the [[Loggerhead Shrike]] hunts from perches and stores prey on thorns or barbed wire; the [[Northern Bobwhite]] needs shrubby cover for its family groups (coveys). The data release says boundary avoidance was "idiosyncratic across species" ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]), with bobwhite avoiding tree-grass boundaries least, exactly what niche differences predict ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]).
