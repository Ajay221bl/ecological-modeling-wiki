# Reintroduction

Bringing wolves back to a national park they were hunted out of a century ago is a reintroduction. A **reintroduction** is a [[Translocation]] that releases a species into part of its historical range from which it had disappeared, to re-establish a wild population there ([[Population]]). Success is not "the animals were released"; it is survival, breeding by the founders, breeding by their offspring and spread into the available habitat, which can take several generations.

That is why monitoring for years matters. [[Happe et al. 2020]] treated "two generations of fishers produced following reintroduction" as their test of reproductive success, and found it: DNA identified founders, first-generation and at least second-generation offspring. Occupancy surveys add the spatial side: whether the population fills the habitat that planners expected and whether its distribution has settled. Happe et al. found fisher occupancy of only 0.08 to 0.24 per year, lower than established populations elsewhere, and a distribution still shifting west and south 5 to 8 years after release ([[Occupancy]], [[Fisher Occupancy Case Study]]).

Reintroduced animals are "naive" to their new home and may need several generations to settle into the best places, so early occupancy patterns may not show long-term habitat preferences ([[Habitat]]). A reintroduction is not the same as an introduction: introducing a species to a place it never lived is a different, riskier act.
