# Translocation

Moving a few hives of bees to an orchard that has none is a translocation of bees. In conservation, a **translocation** is the deliberate capture, transport and release of wild animals into another area, to start, re-establish or boost a population ([[Biological Population]]). [[Happe et al. 2020]] call it "a mainstay of efforts to conserve and restore imperiled or sensitive wildlife species throughout the world", and note that for decades it was often done "with limited planning and post-release monitoring".

Its success hangs on habitat: the release area must provide places to breed and raise young, safety from predators, shelter from heat and cold, and enough food and water ([[Habitat]]). When the species has been gone for decades, that habitat must be judged from studies elsewhere, which is exactly the assumption Happe et al. tested. Monitoring after release, by [[Radio-telemetry Data|telemetry]], cameras, DNA and occupancy surveys, is how you learn whether the animals survived, bred and spread ([[Occupancy]], [[Camera Trap]]).

It is the setting of [[Happe et al. 2020]], where 90 fishers were moved from British Columbia to the Olympic Peninsula in 2008 to 2010 ([[Fisher Occupancy Case Study]]). Translocation is the broader term; a [[Reintroduction]] is the kind of translocation that returns a species to a place where it used to live.
