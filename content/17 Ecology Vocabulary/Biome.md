# Biome
**In one line:** A biome is a huge region of the Earth with a similar climate and the same general type of vegetation, such as desert, grassland or deciduous forest.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Ecosystem]] (a biome contains many ecosystems) · [[Levels of Ecological Organization]]

## Start from zero
Drive west from Arkansas to Colorado. At first you pass through forest broken by farms. In Oklahoma and Kansas the trees thin out and you see wide open prairie. Near the Rockies it gets drier still and the grass gets short. You have crossed from one **biome** to another.

A biome is named for what the land looks like: its dominant plant form. The main land biomes include:
- **Tundra:** cold, treeless, low plants.
- **Desert:** very dry, sparse plants.
- **Temperate grassland:** grasses dominate, few trees (the North American Great Plains).
- **Temperate deciduous forest:** trees that drop their leaves in winter (much of the eastern US).
- **Tropical rainforest:** hot, wet, tall evergreen trees.

## Why it exists
Climate sets the broad limits on what can grow. Two numbers do most of the work: **temperature** and **rainfall**.
- Too little rain, and trees cannot get enough water: you get desert or grassland.
- Enough rain, and trees can outgrow and shade out grasses: you get forest.

The biome idea lets ecologists compare places far apart. Grasslands in Kansas, Argentina and Mongolia have different species but work in similar ways, because similar climates create similar problems for plants.

**The big catch:** climate sets what *can* grow, not always what *does*. Where climate is in between, [[Disturbance]] such as fire and grazing decides whether you get grass or trees.

## A tiny example
Very rough guide for temperate regions (real boundaries depend on temperature, soil and season of rain too):

| Annual rainfall | Typical biome |
|---|---|
| Under about 25 cm | Desert |
| About 25 to 75 cm | Grassland |
| About 75 to 150 cm | Forest possible; grassland only where fire or grazing keeps trees out |

My sites receive about **127 to 137 cm** of rain a year. That is well into "forest possible". So a grassland in central Arkansas is not held open by dryness. It stays grassy only because fire, mowing or other disturbance keeps trees out ([[Why trees take over grasslands without fire]]).

> The rainfall cut-offs in the table are rough teaching numbers, not values from any course reading. Do not cite them in the Report.

## In my project
- Arkansas sits mostly in the **temperate deciduous forest** biome, near its western edge where it meets the grasslands of the southern Great Plains.
- The USGS metadata says spatial covariance was developed "in the Great Plains and Sagebrush biomes of North America" ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]). [[Berry et al. 2026]] tested whether it still works in small, fragmented grasslands inside a forest-climate region. That is the novelty of the study.
- Inside a biome, finer regions are called **ecoregions** ([[Ecoregions]]). My sites span two: the [[Mississippi Alluvial Valley]] and the [[Arkansas River Valley]].
- The Scissor-tailed Flycatcher is a bird of the southern Great Plains grassland, and Arkansas lies roughly at the eastern edge of its main breeding range ([[Scissor-tailed Flycatcher]]).

## Common confusions
- **Biome vs ecosystem.** A biome is a continent-scale category. An ecosystem can be one field.
- **Biome vs ecoregion.** Ecoregions are smaller, local subdivisions based on landform, soils and vegetation.
- **"The map biome is what you will find."** Not always. A forest-biome region can contain grassland where people or fire keep trees out, and farmland where people cleared forest.
- **"Biome boundaries are sharp lines."** They are wide transition zones.
