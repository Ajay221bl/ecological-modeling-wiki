# Ecological Succession

Find an abandoned farm field in Arkansas and come back every few years. In year 1 to 2 it is fast-growing annual weeds such as ragweed and horseweed; by year 3 to 5 perennial grasses and [[Plant Functional Groups|forbs]] such as broomsedge and goldenrod make it look like a meadow; from year 5 to 15 shrubs and vines such as blackberry, sumac and young cedars move in; from year 15 to 40 fast-growing trees (pines, sweetgum) shade out the grass; and after year 40 slower, shade-tolerant oaks and hickories close the canopy. That sequence is **ecological succession**: the gradual, fairly predictable change in which plants, and so which animals, occupy a place after it is cleared. Starting from existing soil, as here, it is *secondary* succession; starting from bare rock or new land such as a lava flow it is *primary* succession, and much slower. (The years and plant names are a typical southeastern old-field sequence from general knowledge, not from a course reading; real timing varies with soil, seed sources and management.)

Why does it happen? Each stage changes conditions in ways that favour the next: weeds and grasses build soil and hold moisture, shrubs grow taller than grass and shade it, and trees grow taller still and shade the shrubs. Taller plants win the race for light unless something knocks them back, so in a climate with enough rain the race ends in forest. [[Disturbance]] (a sudden event such as fire, mowing or flood that removes plants) resets the clock to an earlier stage, so every [[Grassland]] in a humid region is a stage of succession being held in place ([[Why trees take over grasslands without fire]]). A toy example shows the speed-up: start a field at 5% shrub cover and let cover double every 4 years with no disturbance, giving 10% at year 4, 20% at year 8 and 40% at year 12, by which point it is shrubland. A burn at year 4 might knock it back to 2% and restart the count. Real growth is not this neat, but unmanaged change does accelerate, and past some point it can lock in ([[Woody Encroachment]], [[Ecological Resilience and Alternative States]], [[Feedback Loops in Ecology]]). The birds change with the plants: open grass holds Dickcissel, [[Eastern Meadowlark]] and Grasshopper Sparrow; shrubby ground holds Field Sparrow, Yellow-breasted Chat, Indigo Bunting and Common Yellowthroat; forest holds Tufted Titmouse, woodpeckers and vireos.

Four confusions. Succession does not always end in one fixed "climax" forest: that older idea is too tidy, since endpoints depend on soil, climate, chance and disturbance. Later stages are not better: each supports different species, and grassland birds need the early one. Succession is not a strict ladder: stages overlap and can be skipped, as when a nearby cedar seed source sends a field quickly to woody cover. And woody encroachment is not a separate process: it is succession seen from a grassland's point of view. In the grassland bird data several of the most-detected species are shrubby-stage birds, Indigo Bunting (863 point-visits), Yellow-breasted Chat (466), Common Yellowthroat (366) and Field Sparrow (288), a clue that many points sit in or near mid-succession vegetation ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]). The field data include an old-field cover class (OF50) and a scrub class (SC50); in the Oct 13 results all-species [[Species Richness|richness]] was about 4% higher per SD of scrub cover ([[Preliminary Results]]). All sites use mowing, burning and disking to push succession back toward grass ([[Grassland Restoration and Management]]).
