# Ecological Succession
**In one line:** Succession is the gradual, fairly predictable change in which plants (and so which animals) occupy a place after it is cleared, typically from weeds to grass to shrubs to forest in a humid climate.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Disturbance]] (what starts succession over) · [[Grassland]] (one stage along the way)

## Start from zero
Find an abandoned farm field in Arkansas and come back every few years:
- **Year 1 to 2:** fast-growing annual weeds (plants that live one year), such as ragweed and horseweed.
- **Year 3 to 5:** perennial grasses and forbs take over, such as broomsedge and goldenrod. It looks like a meadow.
- **Year 5 to 15:** shrubs and vines move in, such as blackberry, sumac and young cedars. Birds that eat berries drop more seeds.
- **Year 15 to 40:** fast-growing trees (pines, sweetgum) shade out the grass.
- **Year 40 and later:** slower, shade-tolerant trees such as oaks and hickories form a closed forest.

This sequence is **succession**. Starting from an existing soil, as here, it is **secondary succession**. Starting from bare rock or new land (a lava flow) it is **primary succession** and is much slower.

> [!warning] Verify
> The year ranges and plant names above are a typical southeastern old-field sequence from general knowledge, not from my sources. Real timing varies with soil, seed sources and management.

## Why it exists
Each stage changes conditions in ways that favour the next:
- Weeds and grasses build soil and hold moisture.
- Shrubs grow taller than grass and shade it.
- Trees grow taller still and shade the shrubs.

Taller plants win the race for light unless something knocks them back. In a climate with enough rain, the race ends in forest. **Disturbance** (fire, mowing, flood) resets the clock to an earlier stage. Every grassland in a humid region is a stage of succession that is being held in place ([[Why trees take over grasslands without fire]]).

The birds change along with the plants:

| Stage | Typical breeding birds |
|---|---|
| Open grass | Dickcissel, Eastern Meadowlark, Grasshopper Sparrow |
| Shrubby | Field Sparrow, Yellow-breasted Chat, Indigo Bunting, Common Yellowthroat |
| Forest | Tufted Titmouse, woodpeckers, vireos |

## A tiny example
A field has 5% shrub cover today. With no disturbance, imagine shrub cover doubling every 4 years:
- Year 0: 5%
- Year 4: 10%
- Year 8: 20%
- Year 12: 40%

By year 12 it is shrubland, not grassland. A burn in year 4 might knock it back to 2% and restart the count. Real growth is not this neat, but the idea holds: unmanaged change speeds up.

- The most frequently detected species in my data include many **shrubby-stage** birds: Indigo Bunting (863 point-visits), Yellow-breasted Chat (466), Common Yellowthroat (366) and Field Sparrow (288). Their abundance is a clue that many points sit in or near shrubby, mid-succession vegetation, not pure open grass ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]).
- The field data include an **old field** cover class (OF50) and a **scrub** class (SC50); scrub cover (SC50) is a predictor in my habitat models. In the Oct 13 results, all-species richness was about 4% higher per SD of scrub cover, fitting those shrubby-stage birds ([[Preliminary Results]]).
- All sites use mowing, burning and disking to push succession back toward grass ([[Grassland Restoration and Management]]).
- When woody plants advance far enough, the change can lock in ([[Woody Plant Encroachment]], [[Ecological Resilience and Alternative States]]).

## Common confusions
- **"Succession always ends in one fixed 'climax' forest."** That older idea is now seen as too tidy. Endpoints depend on soil, climate, chance and disturbance, and many places never reach a stable end.
- **"Later stages are better."** Each stage supports different species. Grassland birds need the early stage.
- **"Succession is a strict ladder."** Stages overlap and can be skipped. A field with a nearby cedar seed source may jump quickly to woody cover.
- **Succession vs encroachment.** Succession is the general process; [[Woody Plant Encroachment]] is one part of it seen from a grassland's point of view.
