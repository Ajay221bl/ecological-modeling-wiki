# Riparian Zone

Fly over a dry landscape and you can trace every creek by its ribbon of green. A **riparian zone** is the strip of land along a river or stream whose soil and plants depend on the stream's water. Because water is close to the surface, riparian plants grow lush even when the surrounding hills are brown, so these strips hold far more plant and animal species than the dry land around them, often wetland plants found nowhere else nearby. They also shade the water, hold banks together and filter runoff.

That same green attracts grazers. Cattle and elk gather near water and on the best forage, so riparian zones are often grazed much harder than the uplands ([[Grazing]]). That is why the Valles Caldera scientists put their exclosure experiment along riparian zones: that is where grazing damage was worst and where recovery would show first ([[Exclosure]]).

In Homework 1 each column of the dataset after the metadata is a riparian plant species, and the value is how many of 300 points in the plot hit that species. Those hits are a measure of cover, so the [[Species Richness]] of a row is the number of species with at least one hit, and the [[Inverse Simpson Index]] uses each species' share of all hits ([[Diversity Index]]).
