# Habitat Generalist

Think of someone who will eat almost anywhere. Close half the restaurants in town and they barely notice. A **habitat generalist** is a species like that: it can make a living in many kinds of [[Habitat]], because its [[Ecological Niche]] is broad. The Northern Cardinal is a generalist: it nests in shrubs and edges almost anywhere, from forest edges to city gardens, and woods, hedges, yards and fields all work for it. The American Crow and the Red-winged Blackbird are generalists too.

Why does this matter? Because generalists often gain when a specialist's habitat degrades. Compare two points at one refuge. The open field holds 3 grassland specialists and 1 shrub, edge or woodland bird, a richness of 4; the field beside a tree line holds 1 specialist and 6 of the others, a richness of 7. The tree-line point "wins" on total [[Species Richness]], 7 to 4, while holding a third as many grassland specialists: the edge has let generalists in ([[Edge Effects]]). So counting "how many species" without asking "which kind" can reward exactly the change a grassland manager worries about. Generalists are not bad birds; they just do not tell you about grassland quality. The opposite end of the scale is the [[Habitat Specialist]].

In the grassland bird data, the most detected species are mostly generalists and edge birds: Indigo Bunting (863 point-visits), Northern Cardinal (685), Red-winged Blackbird (501) and American Crow (481), all ahead of the first grassland bird, Dickcissel (331). That is why all-species richness rose toward tree-grass edges while focal grassland richness rose toward cores ([[Why all-species and focal-species richness can disagree]], [[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]).
