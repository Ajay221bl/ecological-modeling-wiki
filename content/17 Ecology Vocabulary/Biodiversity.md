# Biodiversity

Picture two gardens: one long row of identical tomato plants, and a plot with tomatoes, beans, sunflowers, bees, ladybugs and a wren in the fence. The second is clearly more diverse. **Biodiversity** puts that feeling into numbers: it is the variety of life, counted at three levels. Genetic diversity is variety within one species (not all Dickcissels are genetically the same); species diversity is how many [[Species and Scientific Names|species]] a place holds and how evenly they are represented; and ecosystem diversity is how many kinds of [[Ecosystem]] a region contains, such as grassland, forest and wetland. Species diversity has two parts: *richness*, the number of species ([[Species Richness]]), and *evenness*, whether they are about equally common or one dominates. It is usually measured on an [[Ecological Community]], all the species living together in one place.

Why do both parts matter? Take two fields, each with 4 species and 100 birds. Field A has 25 Dickcissels, 25 [[Eastern Meadowlark|meadowlarks]], 25 bobwhites and 25 Field Sparrows; Field B has 97 Dickcissels and one each of the others. Richness is the same, 4, but A is perfectly even and B is dominated by one species, so most people and most diversity indices would call A more diverse ([[Diversity Index]], [[Inverse Simpson Index]]). Richness alone cannot see the difference. Ecologists also separate diversity by [[Spatial Scale|spatial scale]]: *[[Significance Level|alpha]]* diversity at one spot (one survey point), *beta* diversity, how different the spots are from each other, and *gamma* diversity, the total across a region. These need not move together: making every site a bit of everything raises alpha but can lower gamma if specialists vanish everywhere.

The main lesson for the grassland bird project is that more is not automatically better: a place can gain species while losing the ones that matter most, so measuring diversity carefully means asking which species, and where. The project's response is richness at a point-visit, an alpha measure that ignores evenness and identity. All-species richness was higher near edges (Spearman −0.41 with [[Spatial Covariance|spatial covariance]] at the largest window, where negative covariance marks a tree-grass boundary), because both grassland and woodland birds can be detected there ([[Edge Effects]], [[EDA Findings]]); focal richness went the other way (+0.26 at the smallest window). On the 376 model points Camp Robinson has the highest all-species richness (7.58 per visit) and among the fewest focal species (0.25), while Bald Knob has the lowest richness (4.73) and the most focal species (0.99) ([[Why all-species and focal-species richness can disagree]], [[Focal Species]]). So a higher richness number could mean a more degraded grassland, if the extra species are edge [[Habitat Generalist|generalists]] ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]). Three confusions: biodiversity is not just richness; more species is not better habitat for a target group; and species detected undercount species present ([[Why observed richness is not true richness]]). The conservation concern behind the study is a loss of birds, not just species: North American bird populations have fallen steeply since 1970, grassland birds more than most ([[Rosenberg et al. 2019]], [[Grassland Bird Decline]]; check the exact figures before quoting).
