# Grazing

A lawn mowed once a week stays green; a lawn mowed every day down to the soil turns to dirt. **Grazing** is animals eating grasses and other low plants, and the effect depends on how hard and how long. Moderate grazing can even keep a meadow diverse, because grazers trim the fast-growing dominant grasses and give smaller plants light. **Overgrazing** is too many grazers for too long: plants are bitten before they can regrow, roots shrink, bare soil appears, and the number of plant species falls ([[Species Richness]]).

The grazers that matter in the course homework are ungulates, the hoofed mammals: cattle and sheep (livestock), and wild deer and elk. In the Valles Caldera of New Mexico, heavy sheep and cattle grazing during Spanish and then American ownership stripped the riparian meadows of many native species ([[Riparian Zone]]). Fencing grazers out with an [[Exclosure]] is the standard way to measure how much grazing changes a plant community, because the same site can be watched with and without the grazers.

Grazing effects are slow and cumulative. One summer without cattle may change little; ten summers may change a lot. That is why Homework 1 asks whether the treatment effect changes with year, an [[Interaction Term]] between treatment and time, and why the [[Interaction Plot]] is the natural first look at the data.
