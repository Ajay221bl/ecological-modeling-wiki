# Grassland

Picture a hayfield in summer: knee-high grass, scattered purple and yellow flowers, insects buzzing, and no trees except at the far edge. A **grassland** is open land dominated by grasses and wildflowers with few or no trees; in North America a natural grassland is usually called a *prairie*. Its plants come in a few kinds. *Grasses* (graminoids) have narrow leaves and jointed stems, and grass-like sedges and rushes are counted with them; *forbs* are non-grass flowering plants, the "wildflowers" such as coneflowers, milkweed and goldenrod. Native prairie plants are mostly *perennials*, living for years and regrowing each spring from deep roots. *Warm-season grasses* such as big bluestem, switchgrass and Indian grass grow most in summer heat, and many native prairie grasses are of this kind; *cool-season grasses* such as fescue grow most in spring and fall, and many pasture and lawn grasses are cool-season and non-native.

Why is a grassland grass and not forest? Trees grow taller and shade grass out, so in a wet enough place trees should win, and grassland persists only when something stops them. Dryness does it in the western Great Plains, where there is not enough rain for many trees. Fire does it because grasses keep their growing points at or below ground and resprout after a burn while young trees are killed. Grazing animals such as bison ate and trampled seedlings ([[Grazing]]), and people now do some of the job by mowing, [[Mowing|haying]] and disking ([[Disturbance]], sudden events that remove plants). Remove all four in a humid climate and a grassland turns to shrubs, then woods ([[Ecological Succession]], [[Woody Encroachment]]). To check whether a patch is grassland, lay a 1 m by 1 m square on the ground and estimate what fraction each plant group covers seen from above: say grass 65%, forbs 20%, shrubs 5% and bare ground 10%. That is [[Percent Cover]]; grass plus forbs (85%) dominate and woody plants are low, so this square is grassland. Satellites estimate the same thing for each [[Pixels and Spatial Resolution|30 m pixel]]. A grassland is one kind of [[Ecosystem]] and the defining vegetation of the temperate grassland [[Biome]].

Four confusions. Any grass is not grassland: a mowed lawn, a fescue pasture and a native prairie are all grassy but offer very different habitat, because height, density and plant species matter to birds. Grassland is not empty land: native prairie can hold over a hundred plant species in a small area, and its root mass can exceed what is above ground. Grassland is not savanna, which has scattered trees over grass, in between grassland and woodland. And in a humid climate, grassland is a state that must be maintained. Grasslands matter because a whole set of birds, insects and plants is built for open ground, and most of the original tallgrass prairie has been ploughed into cropland (the widely repeated "over 90% lost" comes from no course reading here, so check a source before citing a number; [[Grassland Bird Decline]], [[Brennan and Kuvlesky 2005]]). All five project sites are patchy remnant grasslands surrounded by forest or farmland ([[Study Sites]], [[Patch, Matrix and Corridor]]). The field data record grassland-like cover classes within 50 m of each point, native/warm-season grass (NG50), cool-season grass (CG50), grass mix (GM50) and old field (OF50), plus lawn (LA50), which is grass but not grassland habitat. The [[Rangeland Analysis Platform]] lumps long-lived grasses and forbs into one group, perennial forb and grass cover (PFG), which [[Spatial Covariance]] compares with tree cover ([[Plant Functional Groups]]). With 127 to 137 cm of rain a year, Arkansas grasslands depend on management to stay open ([[Grassland Restoration and Management]], [[Grassland Core Habitat]]).

Why can a grassland in central Arkansas not stay grassland on its own, while one in western Kansas largely can?
??
Arkansas is wet enough for trees, so without fire, mowing or other disturbance trees take over. Western Kansas is dry enough that trees struggle even without disturbance.
