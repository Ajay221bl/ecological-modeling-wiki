# Edge Effect

The edge of a wood next to a field is brighter, windier and brushier than the middle of the wood, and it has a mix of woodland and field animals. An **edge effect** is a change in conditions, species or ecological processes near the boundary between two different habitats, compared with the interior of either ([[Habitat]]). Edges can be good for a species that needs two habitats close together, for example cover in one and food in the other, and bad for one that needs deep interior, for example a bird whose nests are raided by predators that hunt along edges.

To test an edge effect in a model, you can use distance to the edge as a covariate: a negative coefficient means the response falls as you move away from the edge ([[Explanatory Variable]], [[Regression Coefficient]]). This distance has the advantage of not depending on how big a buffer you draw around each site ([[Spatial Scale]]).

[[Happe et al. 2020]] found the strongest predictor of fisher occupancy was distance to the boundary between protected wilderness and more intensively managed forest, on both sides (model-averaged β = −1.37 ± 0.40 on the logit scale), with an evidence ratio of 254 for including it. They suggest fishers used the edge because managed forests held more snowshoe hares and mountain beavers to eat, while old wilderness forest gave dens and rest sites ([[Fisher Occupancy Case Study]], [[Evidence Ratio]]). An edge effect is a pattern, not an explanation: the prey idea was a suggestion that the authors said needs its own study.
