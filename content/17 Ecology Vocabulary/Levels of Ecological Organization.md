# Levels of Ecological Organization

Think of an address: person, house, street, town, state, country. Each line contains the one before it, and the questions differ by line: "Is this person healthy?" is not "Is this town growing?" Nature has a similar nested set, the **levels of ecological organization**. An *individual* is one living thing, one Dickcissel. A [[Biological Population|population]] is all individuals of one species in one area. An [[Ecological Community]] is all the populations of different species living together. An [[Ecosystem]] is the community plus its non-living surroundings: soil, water, fire, weather. A *landscape* is a mosaic of several ecosystems side by side ([[Landscape Ecology]]). A [[Biome]] is a huge region with similar climate and vegetation, and the *biosphere* is all life on Earth. These are the levels [[Ecology]], the science of where living things are and why, works at.

| Level | Example at one survey point | A question asked there |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | one Eastern Meadowlark singing on a post | Did this bird find a mate? |
| Population | all the meadowlarks using that refuge this summer | Are meadowlarks here increasing? |
| Community | the meadowlark plus every other bird species, say 6 | How many species share this field? |
| Ecosystem | those birds plus grass, insects, soil, rain and last spring's burn | What happens when it floods or burns? |
| Landscape | the field plus the woods on one side and the rice field on the other | How do woods, fields and farms fit together? |

*What to look at:* the same spot tells five different stories depending on the level you choose. Why keep the levels apart? First, different levels have different properties: a single bird lives or dies, a population can grow by 5% a year, a community can hold 6 species or 12. Only a community has a species count and only a population has a growth rate, so mixing levels leads to nonsense like "the species richness of one bird". Second, causes often come from a level up and effects show a level down. Fire is an ecosystem process: it shapes which plants grow (community), which shapes which birds can nest (population), which shapes whether a given bird raises young (individual).

Three confusions. A higher level is not more important: each answers different questions, and conservation needs all of them. Level is not size: a population of ants can live in one log while a community can span a continent, because level means the kind of unit, not its area; how big an area you look at is a separate idea, [[Spatial Scale]]. And a pattern at one level need not hold at another: richness can rise near edges while the six focal grassland species fall ([[Why all-species and focal-species richness can disagree]]). The grassland bird project spans several levels. Its response, [[Species Richness]] at a point-visit, is a community measure; [[Berry et al. 2026]] modelled each of six declining species inside one [[Multi-species Occupancy Model]], combining species into a community picture; and [[Spatial Covariance]] is a landscape measure, computed from satellite pixels in a [[Moving Window Analysis|moving window]] (90 m, 270 m or 810 m on a side) around each point, with every site sitting in a forested or agricultural matrix ([[Patch, Matrix and Corridor]]). So the model links a landscape [[Predictor Variable|predictor]] to a community response, which stops it from claiming something about individual birds ("birds choose to avoid trees") that richness alone cannot show.
