# Scissor-tailed Flycatcher

Picture a utility wire along a pasture in Oklahoma. A slim gray bird sits on it with a tail longer than its body, split into two streamers like an open pair of scissors. It drops off, snaps a grasshopper out of the air, twists and flies back. That is a **Scissor-tailed Flycatcher** (*Tyrannus forficatus*, [[Four-Letter Species Codes|code]] STFL). A *flycatcher* hunts flying insects by sallying out from a perch, so it needs open space where insects fly and a perch to watch from: a wire, fence post or lone tree, which is also where it nests. Its range is centred on the southern Great Plains, Arkansas sits near the eastern edge of where it commonly breeds, and it winters mainly in Mexico and Central America. It is a kingbird (genus *Tyrannus*), a close relative of the [[Eastern Kingbird]], and one of the six [[Focal Species]] of the grassland bird project.

It shows that "grassland bird" does not mean "no trees at all". A lone tree in a field is a perch in open country, while a forest edge is the start of closed woods where the open hunting space ends, so a species can use single trees and still avoid tree-grass boundaries ([[Why grassland birds avoid trees]], [[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]); Berry's thesis summary reports strong boundary avoidance (check in [[Berry et al. 2026]]). Near a range edge birds are scarcer and patchier, which makes the data thin, and thin data make jumpy estimates. Scissortails were found at 22 of 416 points, 5.3%; [[Dickcissel]] at 164, 39.4%. If 2 more points had a scissortail, its share would move to 24 / 416 = 5.8%, a jump of almost a tenth of its size; for Dickcissel, 2 more points move 39.4% to 39.9%, barely anything. That is why a [[Multi-species Occupancy Model]] lets rare species borrow strength from the community ([[Partial Pooling]]).

In the project data it was on 32 of 1,250 point-visits (ground detections only) at 22 points, 46 birds: BK 12, HB 10, HO 5, CR 4, DC 1, found at all five sites but never often, and all within 100 m, the radius the occupancy analysis used. Because scissortails like wires and fence lines, some detections may come from [[Roadside Surveys]] points on the perimeter where wires run, worth remembering whenever roadside and grid points are compared ([[Systematic Grid Sampling]]). For [[Species Richness]] a scissortail adds 1 species, the same as a crow; for focal richness it is one of only six that count. Two confusions: using trees does not mean liking woodland edges, and a "strong" response means a clear direction, not many detections; it still rests on 22 points. Range and wintering details should be checked in a species account before citing.
