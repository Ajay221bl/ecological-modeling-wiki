# Scissor-tailed Flycatcher
**In one line:** A pale gray bird with salmon-pink sides and an extremely long forked tail (*Tyrannus forficatus*, code STFL) that perches on wires and lone trees over open country and catches insects in the air.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Focal Species]] (why it is one of the six) · [[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]] (it is a grassland bird that still needs perches)

## Start from zero
Picture a utility wire along a pasture in Oklahoma or Texas. A slim gray bird sits on it. Its tail is longer than its body and splits into two streamers, like an open pair of scissors. It drops off the wire, snaps a grasshopper out of the air, twists, and flies back. That is a Scissor-tailed Flycatcher.

A **flycatcher** is a bird that hunts flying insects by sallying out from a perch and returning. To do that it needs two things:
- **Open space** where insects fly and can be seen.
- **A perch** to watch from: a wire, a fence post or a lone tree.

It also nests in that lone tree, a shrub or even on a utility pole.

Its range is centred on the southern Great Plains (Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas). Arkansas sits near the eastern edge of where it commonly breeds. It is a migrant and spends winter mainly in Mexico and Central America.

## Why it exists (why it matters here)
**"Grassland bird" does not mean "no trees at all".** The scissortail needs some vertical structure. But a **lone tree in a field** is very different from a **forest edge**. A lone tree is a perch in open country. A forest edge is the start of closed woods, where the open hunting space ends. So a species can use single trees and still avoid tree-grass boundaries ([[Why grassland birds avoid trees]]).

**Near its range edge.** Near the edge of a species' range, birds are usually scarcer and patchier. That makes a dataset from Arkansas thin for this species.

> [!warning] Verify
> The thesis summary reports that Scissor-tailed Flycatcher responded **strongly** to tree-grass boundaries (avoided them; all six compared in [[Focal Species]]). Treat this as approximate until checked in [[Berry et al. 2026]]. Range and wintering details are from general knowledge; check a species account (for example Birds of the World) before citing them.

## A tiny example
How thin are the data? Scissortails were found at 22 of 416 points, about 5%. Compare [[Dickcissel]] at 164 of 416, about 39%.

Now imagine the model estimates "the share of points with a scissortail" from those counts. If 2 more points had a detection, the share would move from 22/416 = 5.3% to 24/416 = 5.8%, a jump of almost a tenth of its size. For Dickcissel, 2 more points move 39.4% to 39.9%, barely anything. Small counts make estimates jumpy. This is why a [[Multi-species Occupancy Model]] lets rare species borrow strength from the community ([[Partial Pooling]]).

## In my project
Scissor-tailed Flycatcher was found at 32 point-visits (out of all 1,250, ground detections only) at 22 points, with 46 birds counted. By site: BK 12, HB 10, HO 5, CR 4, DC 1. It was found at all five sites, unlike the meadowlark, but never often. All 46 birds were recorded within 100 m, the radius the occupancy analysis used.

Because scissortails like wires and fence lines, some detections may come from [[Roadside Surveys]] points, which sit on the perimeter where wires run. Roads also create a strip of open edge. That is worth remembering whenever roadside and grid points are compared ([[Systematic Grid Sampling]]).

For my [[Species Richness]] response, a scissortail adds 1 species to a point-visit, the same as a crow. For focal-species richness, it is one of only six species that count.

## Common confusions
- **"Uses trees, so it should like woodland edges."** It uses **single** trees in open country; a forest wall removes the open space it hunts in.
- **"Strong response means many detections."** It means a clear direction. The estimate still rests on only 22 points.
- **"Flycatchers are one family of similar birds."** The scissortail is a kingbird (genus *Tyrannus*), a close relative of the [[Eastern Kingbird]].
