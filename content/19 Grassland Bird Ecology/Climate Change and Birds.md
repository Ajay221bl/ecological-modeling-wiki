# Climate Change and Birds
**In one line:** The long-term warming of Earth, and the shifts in rain, drought and extreme weather that come with it, reach birds through their timing, their food, their habitat and even their body size.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Ecosystem]] (how weather, plants and animals are linked) · [[Breeding Season Timing]] (why the calendar matters to a nesting bird)

## Start from zero
**Weather** is what is happening today: hot, windy, raining. **Climate** is the long-run pattern of weather in a place, usually summarised over about 30 years: the usual temperatures, the usual rainfall, how often droughts come. A common saying: climate is what you expect, weather is what you get.

Since the late 1800s, Earth's average surface temperature has risen by a little over 1 °C, mainly because burning fossil fuels has added heat-trapping gases such as carbon dioxide (CO2) to the air. One degree sounds small. But an average hides the extremes: more very hot days, heavier downpours in some places, longer droughts in others.

## Why it exists
Why should a bird care about one degree? Four routes, each easy to picture:

1. **Timing mismatch.** Many birds time nesting partly by day length, which climate does not change. The insects their chicks eat respond to temperature, which it does. In a warm spring the caterpillar peak comes early, and chicks can hatch after the feast is over ([[Breeding Season Timing]]).
2. **Range shifts.** As places warm, the area with a suitable climate for a species moves, often toward the poles or uphill. Species that cannot follow, or whose habitat cannot follow, lose ground.
3. **Habitat change.** Drought, heat and changing fire seasons change plants. Rising CO2 is one suggested reason trees and shrubs are gaining on grasses in some grasslands ([[Woody Plant Encroachment]]), though land use and fire suppression are the better-established drivers.
4. **Bodies.** Heat is hard on small animals. Ground nests in open grassland have no shade, so heat waves during nesting can kill eggs and chicks.

The fourth route can be subtle. [[Shipley et al. 2022]] used 30 years of tree swallow data and found adult birds getting structurally smaller as the climate warms, even though warmer nests grow bigger chicks. Selection, not just growth conditions, seems to be shaping body size.

## A tiny example
Made-up dates for one bird and its food:

| | Insect peak | Chicks hatch | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past | May 20 | May 25 | 5 days |
| Warmer now | May 10 | May 25 | 15 days |

The bird did nothing different. The insects moved 10 days earlier. The chicks now hatch 15 days after peak food instead of 5, when caterpillars are scarcer and older.

## In my project
The metadata ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]) gives the study's climate: temperatures of about 15.5 to 35 °C during the surveys, and 127 to 137 cm of rain a year. That is far wetter than the western Great Plains, which helps explain why trees invade Arkansas grasslands so readily.

What my data can and cannot say:
- **Cannot:** anything about climate change. Two years, one per site, is weather, not climate.
- **Weather matters anyway.** Counts were done only with wind under 19.3 km/h and no rain, and I include Wind and Sky in the model because weather changes how easily birds are detected ([[Why include wind and sky]], [[Detection Probability]]).
- **Year is tied to site.** Any difference between 2022 and 2023 weather is bundled with the site differences ([[Why site and year cannot be separated]]).
- **Floods.** Sites were often partly flooded into early summer. Unreachable flooded grid points were dropped, and flooded pixels were masked out of the spatial covariance maps ([[Flood Masking]]).

## Common confusions
- **"A cold spring disproves climate change."** That is one season's weather, not the 30-year pattern.
- **"Warming only matters in summer."** Spring timing, winter survival and drought all matter.
- **"Birds just move north."** Only if habitat, food and nest sites can move with them.

> Why can warming hurt chicks even if the parent birds nest on exactly the same date as before?
> ?
> Their insect food responds to temperature and peaks earlier in warm springs. If the parents still time nesting by day length, the chicks hatch after the food peak, so there is less to eat.
