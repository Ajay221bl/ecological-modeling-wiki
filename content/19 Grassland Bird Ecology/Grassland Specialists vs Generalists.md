# Grassland Specialists vs Generalists
**In one line:** A specialist can only live in a narrow kind of place (open grass), while a generalist can make a living in many kinds of places, so the two react very differently when a grassland changes.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Habitat]] (where a species lives) · [[Ecological Niche]] (what it needs to live there)

## Start from zero
Think of two people looking for a place to eat. One will only eat at a single kind of restaurant. The other will eat almost anywhere. Close half the town's restaurants and the picky eater may go hungry, while the easy eater barely notices.

Birds are the same. A **specialist** has a narrow [[Ecological Niche]]: it needs one kind of habitat. A **generalist** has a broad one: woods, hedges, yards and fields all work.

A **grassland specialist** needs open grass to nest and feed, and usually avoids trees. An Eastern Meadowlark is one. A Northern Cardinal is a generalist: it nests in shrubs and edges almost anywhere, from forest edges to city gardens.

Ecologists often split grassland birds further. **Obligate** grassland birds depend on grassland for nearly everything. **Facultative** ones use grassland but also use shrubs, scattered trees or other open habitats.

> [!warning] Verify
> The obligate and facultative split is usually traced to Vickery et al. (1999), the same work the metadata cites for "grassland-dependent". Check which category that source puts each focal species in before writing it in the Report. My placements below are from general knowledge.

## Why it exists
**It predicts who loses.** When grassland shrinks or fills with trees ([[Woody Plant Encroachment]]), specialists lose their only home. Generalists often move in, because edges and shrubs suit them. That is part of why grassland birds have declined more than most other groups ([[Grassland Bird Decline]]).

**It changes what a count means.** Because generalists gain at edges, total [[Species Richness]] can go **up** as a grassland gets worse ([[Edge Effects]]). Counting "how many species" without asking "which kind" can reward exactly the change you are worried about.

**"Specialist" depends on the habitat.** A Yellow-breasted Chat is not a generalist; it is a **shrubland** specialist. In a grassland study it still counts as a non-grassland bird, and its presence signals shrubs moving in.

## A tiny example
Two points at the same refuge:

| | Open field | Field beside a tree line |
|---|---|---|
| Grassland specialists | 3 | 1 |
| Shrub, edge and woodland birds | 1 | 6 |
| Total richness | 4 | 7 |

The tree line point "wins" on total richness, 7 to 4, but holds a third as many grassland specialists. If I only looked at the total, I would rank the worse grassland as better.

## In my project
The most frequently detected species in my data, in point-visits out of all 1,250 (ground detections only), are mostly not grassland birds:

| Yellow-breasted Chat | 466 | shrub specialist |
| Common Yellowthroat | 366 | wet shrub and tall grass |
| Tufted Titmouse | 350 | woodland |
| Dickcissel | 331 | grassland (focal) |
| Field Sparrow | 288 | old field and shrubby grassland |
| Northern Bobwhite | 202 | grassland and brush (focal) |

The sites tell the same story (Oct 13 run, 376 model points). BK has the lowest all-species richness (4.73) but the most [[Focal Species]] per point-visit (0.99). CR has the highest all-species richness (7.58) but among the fewest focal species (0.25). The models agree: all-species richness rose toward tree-grass edges, while focal richness rose toward cores and fell about 45% per SD of tree cover ([[Spatial Covariance]], [[Preliminary Results]]). The two responses pull in opposite directions ([[Why all-species and focal-species richness can disagree]]).

## Common confusions
- **"Generalists are bad birds."** They are not; they just do not tell you about grassland quality.
- **"Every bird in a field is a grassland bird."** Many are visitors from the edge.
- **"Specialist and generalist are fixed boxes."** It is a sliding scale. Bobwhite sits in the middle: it needs grass but also shrubs.

> [!question]- Check yourself
> Why can woody encroachment raise total species richness at a point even while the grassland gets worse?
> ?
> Trees and shrubs bring in edge, shrub and woodland generalists, which add species to the count, while grassland specialists leave. The total goes up but the grassland birds go down.
