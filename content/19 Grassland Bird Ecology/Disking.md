# Disking
**In one line:** Pulling a row of angled steel discs behind a tractor to slice and turn the top few centimetres of soil, which opens bare ground and sets the plants back to the earliest stage of regrowth.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Ecological Succession]] (disking restarts it near the beginning) · [[Disturbance]] (disking is a strong, soil-level disturbance)

## Start from zero
A **disk harrow** is a frame holding a row of round, slightly cupped steel discs, a bit like a line of dinner plates set on edge. As a tractor pulls it, the discs cut into the soil and flip it. Farmers use it to break up ground before planting.

Wildlife managers borrow it, usually as **light disking**: a shallow pass in strips, not ploughing a whole field. What happens next?
- The dead grass mat (thatch) is chopped and mixed in.
- Bare soil appears between plants.
- Seeds that were lying dormant in the soil (the **seed bank**) get light and sprout. These are mostly fast-growing annual "weeds" and wildflowers such as ragweed and partridge pea.

In a few weeks the strip looks like a young, weedy old field.

## Why it exists
Every disturbance resets [[Ecological Succession]], but by different amounts. A rough ladder:

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Bare soil |
| 1 | Annual weeds and wildflowers |
| 2 | Perennial grasses |
| 3 | Shrubs and saplings |
| 4 | Forest |

| Tool | Where a stage-3 field ends up |
|---|---|
| [[Prescribed Fire]] | About stage 2: grass survives, small woody stems are killed or set back |
| [[Mowing]] | Stage 2 to 3: shorter, but woody plants resprout |
| Disking | Stage 0 to 1: the soil and roots are torn up |

Disking hits the plants hardest because it reaches the soil. That makes it useful for three jobs:
- **Brood habitat for quail.** [[Northern Bobwhite]] chicks eat insects and need to walk between plants. A disked strip full of annual weeds has bugs, seeds and bare ground at chick height. This is a classic bobwhite management practice.
- **Variety.** Strips at stage 1 next to grass at stage 2 make a patchwork that suits more species ([[Habitat Selection]], [[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]).
- **Firebreaks.** A disked line has no fuel, so it stops a [[Prescribed Fire]] where the plan says.

It has downsides. It can favour invasive weeds, cause erosion on slopes, and damage established native perennial grasses if overdone. And it cannot remove a grown tree: a disk does not cut through a 20 cm trunk.

## A tiny example
A made-up 4 ha field, split into 4 strips of 1 ha. The manager disks one strip per year in rotation.
- Year 1: strip A disked. A is stage 0 to 1; B, C, D are grass.
- Year 2: strip B disked. A is now 1 year old (weedy, buggy); B is fresh.
- After 4 years each strip has been disked once, and at any time the field holds strips of 4 different ages.

One tool, used on a schedule, turns one uniform field into four kinds of cover.

## Common confusions
- **"Disking = ploughing."** Light disking is shallow and done in strips; ploughing turns the whole field over deeply.
- **"Disking removes trees."** It sets back seedlings and small stems only.
- **"More disking is always better for birds."** Too much converts grassland into weedy bare ground that grassland specialists avoid.

> [!question]- Check yourself
> Why does disking reset succession further back than mowing does?
> ?
> Mowing only cuts the tops, so roots, including woody roots, survive and regrow. Disking tears into the soil and roots, leaving bare ground where annual weeds sprout from the seed bank, so the field restarts near the very first stage.
