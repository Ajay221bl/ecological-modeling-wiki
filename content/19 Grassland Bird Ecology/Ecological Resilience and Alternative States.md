# Ecological Resilience and Alternative States

Bend a plastic ruler a little and it springs back; bend it hard and it snaps and stays broken. Ecosystems can behave like that. Picture a ball resting at the bottom of a bowl, with a second bowl next door over a ridge. Each bowl is an **alternative state**: a stable way the same land can be, so that the same soil and rain could hold [[Grassland|prairie]] or forest. **Resilience** is the size of the bowl, how big a push the system can take and still roll back; the ecologist C. S. Holling made the idea famous in 1973. (A second, narrower meaning, how *fast* a system bounces back, also gets used; a fast bounce is not the same as a wide bowl.) Rolling over the ridge into the other bowl is a [[Regime Shift|regime shift]] ([[Regime Shifts and Spatial Early Warning Signals]]).

Why would the same land have two stable states instead of one? Because each state is held in place by its own [[Feedback Loops in Ecology|feedback loop]], a chain of cause and effect that comes back round to reinforce where it started. In the grass state, grass makes fuel, fuel lets fire spread and fire kills tree seedlings, so grass stays; fire resists any change toward woods, which ecologists call a *negative* (balancing) feedback, where negative means "resists change", not "bad". In the woody state, trees cast shade, shade thins the grass, thin grass cannot carry fire, so more trees survive ([[Woody Encroachment]]). Try a made-up rule: fire carries through a field only if tree cover is below 30%. Two neighbouring fields get the same plan, "burn every 3 years". Field A starts at 10% tree cover, fire carries, and ten years later it is still about 10%: grassland. Field B starts at 40%, fire cannot spread, and ten years later it is 60% or more: woodland. Same land, rain and treatment, different outcomes, only because of where each started. It also shows *hysteresis*: the way back is not the reverse of the way in. Trees may have moved in when fire stopped at 20% cover, but bringing fire back at 60% does nothing ([[Why trees take over grasslands without fire]]).

Three confusions. Resilient does not mean unchanging: a resilient grassland burns and regrows all the time, it just stays grassland. Reversing the cause does not reverse the change once a threshold is passed (hysteresis). And negative feedback is not bad. For managers, keeping a grassland in its bowl is cheap, while hauling it back over the ridge is expensive and may fail ([[Defend the Core]], [[Grassland Restoration and Management]]). Berry's 2024 thesis frames the grassland bird project this way: grassland and woodland are alternative regimes that meet at spatial boundaries, and [[Spatial Covariance]] is strongly negative exactly at such a boundary ([[Why negative spatial covariance marks a tree-grass boundary]]), while near-zero values suggest a core, as long as the open ground is grass rather than crops. The project's data are one snapshot per site, so resilience, which is about response to a push over time, cannot be measured directly; [[Rangeland Health]] assessments and Batpurev's rangeland study ask similar questions ([[Batpurev et al. 2021]]). Which covariance values mark a core is still unresolved; check [[Berry et al. 2026]].
