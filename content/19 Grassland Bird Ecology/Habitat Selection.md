# Habitat Selection

Think about how you chose where to live: first a city, then a neighbourhood, then a building, then which room gets your desk. Each choice happened at a different scale and used different cues: jobs, rent, a quiet street, a window. Birds do something similar. A [[Dickcissel]] flying north in spring picks a region, then a field, then a patch of tall weeds, then a nest spot, reacting at each step to cues such as grass height, nearby trees and whether others are already singing there. **Habitat selection** is the name for this whole process: an animal ends up in some kinds of [[Habitat]] (the places a species can live) more often than it would if it wandered at random. Which conditions a species can live in at all is its [[Ecological Niche]]; selection is how it chooses among the places on offer.

Selection always compares *use* (where the animal is found) with *availability* (what was on offer), because a raw count means nothing alone. Say a refuge is 20% open grassland and 80% other cover, and a surveyor finds a species at 10 spots, 6 of them in grassland. Use is 60% against availability of 20%, a ratio of 3: the species is in grassland three times as often as random wandering would predict, which is evidence of selection. Had it used grassland 2 times in 10 (20%), the ratio would be 1 and no selection would show. In the language of [[Probability]], selection is a difference between the observed share and the share expected by chance. Two warnings come with it. Selection is not always good for the animal: a bird can settle in a field that looks right but is a bad place to raise young, such as a hayfield mowed while nests are active ([[Mowing]], [[Breeding Season Timing]]); ecologists call that an *ecological trap*. And scale matters: a bird may select open grass within a field while tolerating a landscape that is mostly forest, and a study measures selection only at the scales it looks at ([[Spatial Scale]]).

Four confusions: found there does not mean chose it (maybe there was no other option); chose it does not mean good for it (traps exist); one scale does not tell the whole story; and a correlation does not prove selection, since habitat may move together with site, year or date ([[Confounding]], [[Correlation vs Causation]]). Survey data also record where birds were *detected*, which is not the same as where they are ([[Imperfect Detection]]), so an [[Occupancy Model]] separates "is it there?" from "did we notice it?". [[Berry et al. 2026]] asked a selection-style question: is the chance a point is occupied by each of the six [[Focal Species]] related to [[Spatial Covariance]] around it, at windows of 0.81, 7.29 and 65.61 ha? The data release abstract says the community avoided tree-grass boundaries and favoured intact cores at all three scales, with responses "idiosyncratic across species" ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]). The habitat file also has field measures of cues birds might use within 50 m, [[Visual Obstruction (Robel Pole)]] for grass height and density, [[Tree Density]] and [[Percent Cover]] of 13 cover types, though the Robel and tree measures are missing at too many [[Roadside Surveys|roadside points]] to model; Berry's thesis reportedly found satellite and ground measures only weakly related ([[Field vs Remote Vegetation Measures]]).

## Flashcards

A species is found in grassland at 30% of its sightings, and grassland makes up 30% of the area. Is it selecting grassland?
??
No evidence of it. It uses grassland in exactly the share that grassland is available, which is what random wandering would produce. Selection shows up only when use differs from availability.
