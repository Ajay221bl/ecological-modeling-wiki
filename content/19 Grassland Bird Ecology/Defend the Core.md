# Defend the Core

You have one free afternoon to weed a garden. One bed has three small weeds; another is completely overgrown. Most gardeners pull the three weeds first: ten minutes saves the good bed and stops those weeds seeding, while the overgrown bed would take weeks and might never fully recover. **Defend the Core** applies that logic to grassland. The good bed is the [[Grassland Core Habitat|core]], large, still-open grassland far from trees; the overgrown bed is land already lost to [[Woody Encroachment]], the spread of trees and shrubs into grassland. The strategy is to protect the core first, then *grow the core* by restoring the land next to it, working outward like a widening circle.

Why this order? Start with cost. With a made-up budget of $20,000, restoring wooded land at $1,000 per hectare buys 20 ha, a square about 447 m on a side; take off a 50 m edge band all round and the new core is (447 − 100)² ≈ 120,000 m², about 12 ha. Defending an existing core at $50 per hectare (cheap fire to kill seedlings) keeps 400 ha open, about 33 times as much. Next, the chance of success: a core still has the grass fuel that lets fire carry, native seeds in the soil and grassland birds already present, while land that has flipped to woodland is held there by its own feedback (shade, less fuel, weaker fire; [[Feedback Loops in Ecology]], [[Ecological Resilience and Alternative States]]). Finally, spread: trees invade from edges and from scattered seed trees, so every tree left in a core becomes a new seed source and a new edge. Restoring scattered patches far from any core makes small islands that are mostly edge ([[Edge Effects]], [[Area Sensitivity]]). You also need to find the cores, and satellite maps help: [[Spatial Covariance]] between tree cover and grass cover is strongly negative at tree-grass boundaries and near zero inside cores ([[Why negative spatial covariance marks a tree-grass boundary]]).

Three confusions. Defend the Core does not mean ignoring degraded land; that comes second, tackled from the core's edge outward ([[Grassland Restoration and Management]]). A core is not "the biggest field" but intact open grassland away from trees. And defending is not doing nothing: cores need regular fire or [[Mowing]] to stay cores. The study behind the grassland bird project is titled "Finding the (small) cores" ([[Berry et al. 2026]]): it showed spatial covariance can find cores even in fragmented Arkansas grassland. A trap shows up in the site medians of 3×3 spatial covariance: BK 0.0, HB −0.6, HO −11.4, DC −20.8, CR −41.0. BK scores closest to zero and has the most [[Focal Species]] per point-visit (0.99 against 0.55 overall), but BK is open largely because much of it is cropland (soybean and rice cover about 42% of the ground near its points; [[Percent Cover]]), so near zero there means "no tree-grass boundary", not "intact grassland", and five sites tied to years are a pattern to test, not proof ([[Why site identity is a weak test of a habitat hypothesis]]). The point-level test (Oct 13) supported the idea for the six focal species: focal [[Species Richness|richness]] was about 17% higher per [[Standard Deviation|standard deviation]] toward cores at the 0.81 ha window and about 45% lower per standard deviation of tree cover ([[Hypotheses]], [[Preliminary Results]]). All-species richness went the other way, a reminder that a goal must name which birds it serves. The framework is presented in Berry's thesis as used by USDA NRCS; check program names against an NRCS source before citing.
