# Regime Shift

Picture a ball resting in a bowl: push it a little and it rolls back, so it is stable. Now picture two bowls side by side with a ridge between them. Push hard enough and the ball goes over the ridge into the other bowl and stays there, and pushing it back takes much more effort than keeping it in the first bowl would have. A **regime shift** is an ecosystem making that jump: a flip into a different, lasting state ("regime" just means a stable way the system runs). A grassland held open by fire is one bowl; a juniper woodland that shades out grass and stops fire is the other. The two states are the [[Ecological Resilience and Alternative States|alternative states]] of the same land.

What holds each state in its bowl is [[Feedback Loops in Ecology|feedback]], a chain of cause and effect that comes back to its start. Fire keeps grass open, because grass fuels fire and fire kills young trees; but once trees pass a threshold, they shade out the grass that would fuel the fires that would remove them ([[Woody Encroachment]]). A toy version: at 10% tree cover each year's fires pull cover back to about 10%; at 50% fires are too weak and cover jumps to 60% the next year. Somewhere in between is the ridge, the [[Tipping Point|tipping point]]. So the key mark of a regime shift is not that the change is big, but that feedbacks hold the new state and make it hard to undo; a gradual change just follows its driver and reverses when the driver does. Real landscapes are patchy, so parts can flip at different times, and the ridge itself can move as climate or management change.

Because a flip is hard to undo, managers want warning before it happens, which is the idea of a [[Spatial Early Warning Signal]], and they protect intact grassland first ([[Defend the Core]], [[Grassland Core Habitat]]). In the grassland bird project, regime shifts are the background theory: they explain why Dr. Roberts' group cares about [[Spatial Covariance]] and cores, as tools for telling intact grasslands from ones sliding toward woodland ([[Regime Shifts and Spatial Early Warning Signals]]). The project's data cover one year per site, so they cannot show a shift happening ([[Why site and year cannot be separated]]).
