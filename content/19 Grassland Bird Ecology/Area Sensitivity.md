# Area Sensitivity

Picture two hayfields with the same grass. One is 100 hectares (a hectare, ha, is a 100 m × 100 m square); the other is 2 ha, about 140 m on a side, and surrounded by woods. A grassland sparrow's *territory*, the patch one pair defends for itself, might need only about 1 ha, so by arithmetic the small field has room for two pairs. Yet surveys often find the sparrow in the big field and never in the small one. That species is **area-sensitive**: the chance of finding it, or its density, rises with the size of the [[Habitat Patch|patch]] (a piece of suitable habitat surrounded by something else), beyond what territory size alone would explain. Henslow's Sparrow is the classic grassland example; Grasshopper Sparrow and Upland Sandpiper are often described the same way.

Why would a bird reject a patch that is "big enough"? The main reason is geometry. Near a border, predators and nest parasites from the woods reach in, the [[Edge Effects]] that make conditions worse close to a habitat boundary. Suppose birds avoid the outer 50 m of a square patch. A 100 m square (1 ha) then has no usable middle at all; a 200 m square (4 ha) keeps a 100 m square in the middle, 1 ha, so a quarter of it is usable; a 400 m square (16 ha) keeps a 300 m middle, 9 ha or 56%; and an 800 m square (64 ha) keeps 49 ha, 77%. Quadrupling the area from 4 to 16 ha multiplies the usable middle by nine. Small patches lose far more than their size suggests, so what matters is not the mapped area but the [[Grassland Core Habitat|core]], the part far enough from edges. Two more reasons add to the geometry: many songbirds settle near others of their kind, so a lone patch with no singing neighbours looks empty, and many grassland birds treat a tree line as a warning and stay back from it ([[Why grassland birds avoid trees]]). This is why [[Habitat Fragmentation]], one big habitat broken into many small pieces, hurts area-sensitive birds more than the lost area alone would predict.

Three confusions. Area-sensitive does not mean "has a big territory": a species with a small territory can still avoid small patches. Shape matters as much as size: a long thin 16 ha strip has far less core than a 16 ha square. And how strongly a species is area-sensitive varies by region and study, so never quote a minimum [[Landscape Metrics|patch size]] without a source. In the grassland bird project all five sites are patchy remnant grasslands in a forested or farmed [[Patch, Matrix and Corridor|matrix]] (the surrounding non-habitat land), a hard place for area-sensitive birds, and Henslow's Sparrow is not among the six [[Focal Species]]. [[Spatial Covariance]] gets at the same idea from another angle: within a square window around each survey point it asks whether grass and trees are mixed or separated. The windows were 3×3, 9×9 and 27×27 [[Pixels and Spatial Resolution|pixels]] of 30 m, that is 0.81, 7.29 and 65.61 ha, and the data release reports that the smallest window best explained grassland bird occupancy ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]): in a fragmented landscape even small cores seem to matter ([[Why the window size is a choice of scale]], [[Defend the Core]]). The best window is the scale at which a habitat measure best predicts birds, which is related to, but not the same as, the area a bird needs ([[Spatial Scale]]).
