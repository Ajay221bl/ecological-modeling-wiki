# Loggerhead Shrike

Walk along a barbed-wire fence in open farmland: on one barb hangs a grasshopper, on the next a small lizard. A **Loggerhead Shrike** (*Lanius ludovicianus*, [[Four-Letter Species Codes|code]] LOSH), nicknamed the "butcherbird", put them there. It is a robin-sized songbird, gray with a black [[Flood Masking|mask]] and a hooked bill, but its feet are too weak to hold prey while eating, so it spears the catch on a thorn or wire spike and tears at it; the spiked prey also works as a pantry. To live this way it needs short, open grass where it can see insects, mice and lizards; perches to hunt from, such as fences, wires and lone shrubs; and thorny shrubs to nest in and hang prey on. So it is a grassland bird that depends on a light scatter of woody plants but not on woods, one of the six [[Focal Species]] of the grassland bird project, and one with one of the steepest declines across much of North America, especially the East ([[Grassland Bird Decline]]). Suspected causes include loss of pasture and hedgerow ([[Land-Use Change and Agriculture]]), pesticides reducing insect prey and road collisions; a pair also defends a large territory for its size, so shrikes are thinly spread even in good habitat.

Rare, thinly spread birds teach a lesson about detection. In the project data each of the 8 points with a shrike had it on exactly one of its 3 visits. Does that mean the shrike left? Suppose a shrike lives at a point and on any one visit there is a 1 in 3 chance of detecting it: a [[Detection Probability]] of 0.33. The chance of missing it on one visit is 0.67, and on all 3 visits 0.67 × 0.67 × 0.67 = 0.30. So about 30% of points where a shrike really lives would show no shrike at all, and finding it once in three visits is exactly what we would expect. A zero does not mean absent ([[Imperfect Detection]], [[Repeat-Visit Design]]).

The shrike was found on 8 of 1,250 point-visits (ground detections only), one bird each time: BK 4, DC 2, HO 2, all in 2022. Only 4 of the 8 were within 100 m, the radius the occupancy analysis used for focal species ([[Distance Bands]]), so the model had even fewer records than the file suggests. With numbers this small, a [[Multi-species Occupancy Model]] can only estimate shrike responses by borrowing from the other five species ([[Partial Pooling]]), which works only if shrikes respond to habitat roughly as they do ([[Ecological Similarity Assumption]]); Berry's thesis summary reports a strong avoidance of tree-grass boundaries (check in [[Berry et al. 2026]]). Three confusions: it is not a bird of prey but a songbird that acts like one; needing a few shrubs does not mean it likes [[Woody Encroachment]], since dense woody cover removes its hunting ground ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]); and seen once in three visits does not mean passing through. Decline causes and territory size should be checked against a species account before citing.
