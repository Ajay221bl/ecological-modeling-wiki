# Loggerhead Shrike
**In one line:** A gray, black-masked songbird (*Lanius ludovicianus*, code LOSH) that hunts like a small hawk over short grass and stores its prey by spearing it on thorns or barbed wire.

> [!info] Before this, it helps to know
> [[Focal Species]] (why it is one of the six) · [[Imperfect Detection]] (why a rare bird is easy to miss)

## Start from zero
Walk along a barbed-wire fence in open farmland. On one barb hangs a grasshopper. On the next, a small lizard. Someone put them there on purpose. That someone is a Loggerhead Shrike, nicknamed the "butcherbird".

A shrike is a **songbird**, about the size of a robin, gray with a black mask and a hooked bill. It has no talons strong enough to hold prey while eating. So it does the next best thing: it **impales** the catch on a thorn or wire spike, then tears at it. The spiked prey also works as a pantry, and males may show off a well-stocked one.

To live this way a shrike needs:
- **Short, open grass** where it can see insects, mice and lizards on the ground.
- **Perches** to hunt from: fences, wires, lone shrubs.
- **Thorny shrubs or small trees** to nest in and to hang prey on.

So it is a grassland bird that depends on a light scatter of woody plants, but not on woods.

## Why it exists (why it matters here)
**One of the steepest declines.** Loggerhead Shrikes have declined across much of North America, especially in the East. Suspected causes include loss of pasture and hedgerow, pesticides reducing insect prey, and collisions with cars, since they hunt along roadsides ([[Grassland Bird Decline]], [[Land-Use Change and Agriculture]]).

**Needs space.** A shrike pair defends a large territory for its size, so even in good habitat they are thinly spread. A few points will hold one; most will not.

> [!warning] Verify
> The thesis summary reports that Loggerhead Shrike responded **strongly** to tree-grass boundaries (avoided them; all six compared in [[Focal Species]]). Treat this as approximate until checked in [[Berry et al. 2026]]. Decline causes and territory size are from general knowledge; check a species account before citing them.

## A tiny example
Each of the 8 points with a shrike had it on exactly **one** of its 3 visits, never two or three. Does that mean the shrike left?

Not necessarily. Suppose a shrike lives at a point, and on any one visit there is a 1 in 3 chance an observer detects it (the [[Detection Probability]] is 0.33). Then:
- Chance of missing it on one visit = 1 - 0.33 = 0.67.
- Chance of missing it on all 3 visits = 0.67 x 0.67 x 0.67 = 0.30.

So about 30% of points where a shrike really lives would show **no** shrike at all, and finding it once in three visits is exactly what we would expect. "Zero" does not mean "absent" ([[Imperfect Detection]], [[Repeat-Visit Design]]).

## In my project
Loggerhead Shrike was found at 8 point-visits (out of all 1,250, ground detections only) at 8 different points, one bird each time: BK 4, DC 2, HO 2. All 8 were in 2022. None at CR or HB.

When I added up distance bands, only 4 of the 8 birds were within 100 m, the radius the occupancy analysis used for focal species ([[Distance Bands]]). The other 4 were 101 to 400 m away. So the occupancy model had even fewer shrike records than my file suggests.

With numbers this small, a model can only estimate shrike responses by borrowing from the other five species, which is what a [[Multi-species Occupancy Model]] does ([[Partial Pooling]]). That only works if shrikes respond to habitat in roughly the same way as the others ([[Ecological Similarity Assumption]]).

## Common confusions
- **"It is a bird of prey."** It is a songbird that acts like one; it has a hooked bill but weak feet.
- **"Needs shrubs, so it likes woody encroachment."** It needs a few scattered shrubs in open grass. Dense woody cover removes its hunting ground.
- **"Seen once in 3 visits = passing through."** It may live there and simply be missed on 2 visits.
