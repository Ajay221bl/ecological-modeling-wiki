# Eastern Meadowlark

Drive past a pasture in spring and look at the fence posts. A robin-sized bird sits on one, bright yellow underneath with a black "V" across its chest, throws back its head and whistles a slurred "see-you, see-yeeer". That is an **Eastern Meadowlark** (*Sturnella magna*, [[Four-Letter Species Codes|code]] EAME). Despite the name it is not a lark but a member of the blackbird and oriole family, and it spends most of its time walking on the ground, probing for insects and seeds. The female builds her nest in a small dip on the ground, often weaving a roof of grass over it, so meadowlarks need fairly tall, dense grass and plenty of it, open from edge to edge with only fence posts or a lone shrub as song perches. That makes it a textbook grassland *[[Habitat Specialist|specialist]]*, a species that depends on grassland rather than using many habitats ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]]), and one of the six [[Focal Species]] of the grassland bird project.

Why it matters: if any focal species should avoid tree-grass boundaries, it is this one, so it is a test of whether [[Spatial Covariance]] tracks the birds it is meant to track ([[Why grassland birds avoid trees]]). It is also declining: [[Breeding Bird Survey]] trends have pointed down for decades as pastures and hayfields were ploughed, built on or left to grow into woods ([[Grassland Bird Decline]]), and its ground nests are easily destroyed by [[Mowing]] in the nesting season ([[Breeding Season Timing]]). Now look at where it was found. Almost every point was visited 3 times. At BK, 73 of 288 point-visits had a meadowlark (25%); DC 9 of 280 (3%); HB 6 of 252 (2%); HO 2 of 289 (under 1%); CR 0 of 141. One site holds 73 of the 90 meadowlark visits, 81%. That is a strong signal, but about one place, not yet about habitat.

The common confusion is "seen at BK, so BK is good grassland". BK has the median spatial covariance closest to zero (0.0), the highest focal-species rate (0.99 per point-visit) and the lowest all-species [[Species Richness|richness]] (4.73), but about 42% of the ground within 50 m of BK points is soybean or rice ([[Percent Cover]]), and meadowlarks also use pastures, hayfields and crop margins. BK also differs from the other sites in year (2022) and crew ([[Study Sites]]), so with most detections at one site it is hard to tell "meadowlarks like open ground" from "meadowlarks like BK" ([[Why site identity is a weak test of a habitat hypothesis]], [[Confounding]]). The per-point habitat [[Predictor Variable|predictors]] are meant to separate the two, yet even after them all four other properties have fewer focal species than BK ([[Interpreting Poisson Coefficients|rate ratios]] 0.43 to 0.76; [[Preliminary Results]]), so cropland, year or crew could still be part of BK's lead. Berry's thesis summary reports only a weak meadowlark boundary response (80% [[Credible Interval]] overlapping zero), which with most detections at one site may simply mean "not enough contrast", to be checked in [[Berry et al. 2026]].

## Flashcards

73 of the 90 meadowlark visits were at BK. Why is that not enough to conclude meadowlarks prefer open grassland?
??
Site and habitat are tangled: BK differs from the other sites in year, land use and crew. Only a habitat measure taken at each point can show that meadowlarks follow open grass rather than one property.
++++

Why do meadowlarks need tall, dense grass?
??
They nest on the ground, hidden under a woven roof of grass, so they need enough cover to conceal the nest.
