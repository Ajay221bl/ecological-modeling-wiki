# Land-Use Change and Agriculture

Fly over Iowa in summer and you see a checkerboard of corn and soybean fields to the horizon; almost all of it was once tallgrass prairie. Two words help here. *Land cover* is what is physically on the ground: grass, trees, water, bare soil, crops. *Land use* is what people do with it: cropland, pasture, hayfield, lawn, refuge. The same grass cover can be a pasture, a lawn or a refuge, and to a grassland bird those are very different places. **Land-use change** is people turning land from one use to another, and in North America it is the biggest single reason [[Grassland]] and its birds have shrunk ([[Grassland Bird Decline]]).

It reaches birds in four ways. Outright loss: prairie ploughed or built over is gone as nesting habitat (a commonly cited figure is that under about 4% of original tallgrass prairie remains; check the source before quoting). [[Habitat Fragmentation]]: what remains is broken into small patches in a *matrix* of crops, towns or forest ([[Patch, Matrix and Corridor]]), and small patches are mostly edge ([[Edge Effects]]). Intensification: hedgerows removed, fields farmed edge to edge, hay cut earlier and more often ([[Mowing]]), insecticides cutting the insects chicks eat, and native grass swapped for non-native tall fescue. And abandonment: in the humid Southeast, abandoned farms go through [[Ecological Succession]] to forest. Breaking up what remains makes the loss worse than the area suggests. Take a made-up 1,000 ha landscape and assume birds avoid a 50 m band along every edge. Then: 600 ha of grass in one square block about 2,449 m on a side, with a [[Grassland Core Habitat|core]] of (2,449 − 100)² ≈ 552 ha. Now: 200 ha in four 50 ha blocks about 707 m on a side, with core 4 × (707 − 100)² ≈ 147 ha. Grass area fell 67%, but core fell (552 − 147) / 552 ≈ 73%.

Three confusions. Land cover is not land use. Not any grass helps grassland birds: mown lawns and dense fescue serve few specialists. And agriculture does not only destroy habitat: programs that pay farmers to plant grass on cropland, such as the US Conservation Reserve Program, have given birds like the [[Dickcissel]] new places to nest. In the grassland bird project every site is "patchy remnant grasslands interspersed within a forested or agricultural matrix" ([[Roberts et al. 2025]]), spanning the [[Arkansas River Valley]] and the [[Mississippi Alluvial Valley]], the latter once mostly bottomland forest and swamp and now largely row crops ([[Ecoregions]]). The habitat file records land use within 50 m of each point (`CO50` corn, `SO50` soybean, `RI50` rice, `IA50` inactive agriculture, `OF50` old field, `LA50` lawn; full table in [[Percent Cover]]): around BK points soybean (23%) and rice (19%) are the two largest classes. BK has the lowest all-species [[Species Richness|richness]] (4.73) but the most Dickcissel and [[Eastern Meadowlark]] visits and the highest focal mean (0.99), perhaps because big open farm-and-grass landscapes have few of the edge [[Habitat Generalist|generalists]] that pad out richness elsewhere ([[Grassland Specialists vs Generalists]], [[Why all-species and focal-species richness can disagree]]). BK is one site tied to one year, so that is an idea, not a result ([[Confounding]], [[Study Sites]]).
