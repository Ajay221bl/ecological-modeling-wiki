# Eastern Kingbird

Watch a crow fly over a field in June: suddenly a small dark bird shoots up from a fence post, dives at the crow's back and chases it far across the field. That is very often an **Eastern Kingbird** (*Tyrannus tyrannus*, "tyrant tyrant", [[Four-Letter Species Codes|code]] EAKI), a black-and-white bird with a white band at the tip of its tail. It is a *flycatcher*: it watches from a perch and sallies out to catch flying insects, like its close relative the [[Scissor-tailed Flycatcher]]. It favours open land with scattered trees, fence lines, field edges and shorelines, and it is a long-distance migrant that breeds across much of North America and winters in South America, where it eats mostly fruit. Despite the name it is a songbird, not a bird of prey; the "tyrant" is its temper.

Among the six [[Focal Species]] of the grassland bird project it is the most comfortable near trees, since it needs perches and often nests in a tree at a field's edge, so we might expect it to avoid tree-grass boundaries less than a [[Eastern Meadowlark|meadowlark]] does ([[Edge Effects]]). It is also one of the two rarest focal species in the data, and that brings a lesson about uncertainty. Suppose a model estimates how a species' chance of being present changes as boundaries get stronger, with negative meaning "avoids boundaries". Species A, with many detections, gets a best guess of −1.2 and an 80% [[Credible Interval]] of −1.6 to −0.8: clearly negative. Species B, with 9 detections, gets −0.4 with an interval of −1.5 to +0.7. B's best guess is still negative, but the plausible range includes zero and positive values, so the honest reading is "we cannot tell the direction", not "it does not respond". Fewer detections mean a wider interval. Berry's thesis summary reports a weak kingbird response of exactly this kind (interval overlapping zero), to be checked in [[Berry et al. 2026]].

In the project's data the kingbird was found on only 9 of 1,250 point-visits (ground detections only), at 8 points, 10 birds in all: CR 6, DC 2, HO 1, none at BK or HB. CR has the most negative median 3×3 [[Spatial Covariance]] (−41.0, many tree-grass boundaries) and the fewest focal species overall, yet two thirds of kingbird visits came from there, which fits an edge-tolerant bird. But 6 visits is far too few to lean on, and CR was counted earliest in the season (May 16 to 22, 2023), when migrants may still be settling ([[Breeding Season Timing]]), so site, date and habitat are tangled ([[Confounding]]). Two confusions to avoid: a weak result is not "no effect", and edge-tolerant does not mean "not a grassland bird", since it still hunts over open land. A [[Multi-species Occupancy Model]] can borrow strength from commoner species to say more about rare ones like this. Behaviour, range and diet details should be checked in a species account before citing.

## Flashcards

The kingbird's boundary effect has an 80% credible interval that overlaps zero. Does that mean kingbirds ignore tree-grass boundaries?
??
No. It means the data cannot pin down the direction. With only 9 detections, the plausible range is wide enough to include avoiding, ignoring and even preferring boundaries.

Why might the kingbird avoid tree-grass boundaries less than other focal species?

It hunts from perches and often nests in trees at field edges, so it tolerates trees nearby.
