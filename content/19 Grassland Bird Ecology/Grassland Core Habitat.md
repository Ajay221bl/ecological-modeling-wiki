# Grassland Core Habitat

Think of a pizza: the crust runs all around the outside and the middle is the good part. A grassland patch works the same way. The outer strip near trees and roads is the crust, where [[Edge Effects]] (worse conditions near a habitat border, such as predators reaching in from the woods) make life harder for ground-nesting birds, and the middle, far from everything, is the **core**: the part of the patch that stays reliably open and that [[Habitat Specialist|grassland specialists]] depend on most. Thinking in cores changes the conservation question from "how much grass is there?" to "where are the intact cores, and how do we protect and grow them?" ([[Defend the Core]]).

Cores shrink much faster than grass does. Take a 300 m × 300 m field and suppose birds avoid the outer 50 m (a made-up distance for the arithmetic). The core is 200 × 200 = 40,000 m² of the 90,000 m² total, about 44%. Now a line of trees grows north to south through the middle. Each half is about 150 m wide, so its core is (150 − 100) × 200 = 10,000 m², or 20,000 m² for both halves, about 22%. The trees cover only a thin strip, yet [[Home Range|core area]] halved, because each new tree line carves an edge band out of the interior on both sides. That is why [[Habitat Fragmentation]] and [[Woody Encroachment]] are so damaging. Cores can also be spotted from satellites: look at how grass and trees are arranged across a small neighbourhood, and where they are sorted into separate zones there is a boundary, while where trees are absent from a grassy neighbourhood there is a core. That is the idea behind [[Spatial Covariance]] ([[Why negative spatial covariance marks a tree-grass boundary]]).

Four confusions. A core is not the whole grassland but grassland minus the edge band. A grassy point is not a core point if it is surrounded by trees: the neighbourhood matters. Core size is not fixed, since it depends on how wide the edge band is, which differs by species. And "no trees" means core only if the open ground is grassland: a treeless crop field has no tree edge either. That last one bites in the grassland bird project, where BK has the most core-like median spatial covariance (0.0) but much of the open ground near its points is soybean and rice ([[Percent Cover]]). The paper behind the project, "Finding the (small) cores" ([[Berry et al. 2026]]), treated spatial covariance near zero as a core and strongly negative values as a boundary, measured in windows of 0.81, 7.29 and 65.61 ha, and the smallest (a square 90 m on a side, about a city block) explained occupancy best ([[Roberts et al. 2025]], [[Why the window size is a choice of scale]]). The project's Oct 13 models agree: focal grassland [[Species Richness|richness]] was about 17% higher per [[Standard Deviation|standard deviation]] of spatial covariance at the 0.81 ha window and about 45% lower per standard deviation of tree cover, while all-species richness showed no tree effect ([[Hypotheses]], [[Preliminary Results]]). Which exact values count as a core is still unresolved between the metadata, the abstract and the thesis; check the paper before defining it in the Report.

## Flashcards

Why does adding one tree line through the middle of a grassland reduce core area much more than it reduces grass area?
??
The trees take little area, but they create a new edge band on both sides. All of that band stops counting as core, so interior habitat drops sharply.
++++

Does "no trees nearby" always mean grassland core?
??
No. A treeless crop field also has no tree edge. It is core only if the open ground is grassland.
